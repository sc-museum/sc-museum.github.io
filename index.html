The Signal & Cyber Corps Museum Society — formerly the Fort Gordon Historical Museum Society — is a 501(c)(3) of volunteers preserving the history of Americans who served at Fort Eisenhower, formerly Fort Gordon, in the Signal Regiment, and in the Army's Cyber Corps. The Signal Corps Museum on post closed on 25 February 2021 for base construction, and its collection is in storage while the Society raises funds for a new museum building outside the gates. This virtual museum keeps that collection open to the public in the meantime, room by room.

This Month in History

Signal Corps anniversaries for the month, with a door into the room that tells each story.

October
Two October 4thsA pigeon carried a Signal Corps message through gunfire in 1918. In 1957 Signal Corps antennas were the first in the U.S. government to record Sputnik.
Oct11890
Weather

The weather service leaves the Army

On October 1, 1890, President Benjamin Harrison signed the law creating the Weather Bureau in the Department of Agriculture. For about twenty years Signal Corps soldiers had run the nation's weather service from posts across the country. The handover took effect on July 1, 1891.

Oct41918
Signal Corps Pigeon Service

Cher Ami and the Lost Battalion

Cut off in the Argonne Forest and shelled by their own artillery, Maj. Charles Whittlesey's men sent a message by Signal Corps pigeon. Cher Ami reached his loft badly wounded, the shelling stopped, and the battalion was relieved on the night of October 7. His mounted remains are now at the Smithsonian.

Oct41957
Signal Corps Laboratories

Sputnik, heard first at Deal

When the Soviet Union launched Sputnik I, Signal Corps engineers at the Deal Test Area, a satellite campus of Fort Monmouth, New Jersey, were the first U.S. government team to detect and record its signals. They followed 273 orbits over about 500 hours, though no project had been set up for the job.

Oct91909
Army Aviation

The first Army pilots learn to fly

On October 9, 1909, formal flight training began at College Park, Maryland, with Wilbur Wright teaching two Army lieutenants. On October 26 Lt. Frederic Humphreys flew solo, and Lt. Frank Lahm followed minutes later. On October 10, 1911, the Army tried dropping bombs from an airplane for the first time.

All monthOct1918
Hello Girls

The switchboards of Meuse-Argonne

During the Meuse-Argonne offensive, Grace Banker's Signal Corps telephone operators worked the lines from Bar-le-Duc, France. At the height of the fighting they connected 150,000 calls a day. The war ended on November 11, 1918, and in 1977 Congress finally gave the women of the Signal Corps veteran status.

Also this monthAmerican Archives Month: the CECOM History Office archive holds more than a hundred years of Signal Corps history, from Greely's Arctic journal to World War I training-camp photographs.
November
Weather, Wings, and CyberNovember runs from the Signal Service's first telegraphed weather reports in 1870 to the first West Point cadets choosing the new Cyber Branch in 2014.
Nov11870
Signal Service Weather

The Army's Weather Network Goes Live

At 7:35 a.m. on November 1, 1870, Signal Service observer-sergeants at 24 stations took the first synchronized weather readings and sent them by telegraph to Washington, D.C. The stations stretched from Boston to Key West and west to Cheyenne in Wyoming Territory. The network, organized under Chief Signal Officer Albert J. Myer, began the Army's twenty years of national weather reporting.

Nov291911
Army Aviation in Augusta

Army Fliers Winter in Augusta

On November 29, 1911, the Signal Corps aviation school from College Park, Maryland, arrived in Augusta, Georgia, to keep flying through the winter on the Barnes farm. That winter proved unusually harsh: heavy snow fell in January and February 1912. Pilots trained here included Henry H. "Hap" Arnold and Thomas DeW. Milling. The Signal Corps returned to the Augusta area in 1948 at Camp Gordon.

Nov81942
World War II

Operation TORCH Tests the Signal Corps

On November 8, 1942, British and American forces landed in North Africa in Operation TORCH, which the Army's branch history calls the Signal Corps' "first major test of the war." Naval gunfire knocked out many shipboard radios, and the heavy SCR-299 radio trucks were stowed so deep in the holds that only one came ashore in time. Two British communications ships helped fill the gap.

Nov241877
Coastal Signal Stations

Telegraph at the Wreck of the Huron

Early on November 24, 1877, the steamer Huron ran aground off Nags Head, North Carolina. Sgt. S. W. Naylor, the Signal Corps observer at Kitty Hawk, eight miles away, hurried to the scene and set up a telegraph station on the shore. It sent more than 550 messages through December 11. Only 34 of the 132 men aboard survived.

Nov81966
Vietnam / Medal of Honor

Capt. Euripides Rubio, Communications Officer

On November 8, 1966, during Operation ATTLEBORO in Tay Ninh Province, Vietnam, Capt. Euripides Rubio was communications officer of the 1st Battalion, 28th Infantry. Wounded three times, he took command of a rifle company, then carried a misplaced marking smoke grenade toward the enemy so the air strikes would fall on the enemy, not his men. He received the Medal of Honor posthumously.

Nov202014
Cyber Branch

First Cadets Branch Cyber

On November 20, 2014, at Branch Night at the U.S. Military Academy, 15 cadets of the Class of 2015 received assignments as Cyber officers (17A). They were the first West Point cadets to branch directly into the new Cyber Branch, created that September 1. The next night they met Army Cyber Command and Cyber School leaders, including Cyber School Commandant Col. Jennifer Buckner.

Also this monthBeginning in November 1917, the Signal Corps recruited women from commercial telephone companies, plus bilingual volunteers, as telephone operators; they became the Signal Corps Female Telephone Operators Unit, better known as the "Hello Girls."
December
From Radar to OrbitSignal Corps radar spotted the attackers approaching Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, and seventeen years later, on December 18, 1958, the Corps launched the world's first communications satellite.
Dec91941
Camp Gordon

Camp Gordon Opens for Business

Two days after Pearl Harbor, on December 9, 1941, camp commander Col. Herbert W. Schmid moved his small staff from a temporary office in Augusta's post office to the unfinished headquarters building at Camp Gordon. That month the 4th Infantry Division began moving in. The camp hosted three divisions in World War II and became a Signal Corps training center in 1948.

Dec71941
World War II / Radar

Radar Warning at Opana

At 7:02 a.m. on December 7, 1941, Signal Corps Pvts. Joseph L. Lockard and George A. Elliott, still running their radar at Opana on Oahu's northern tip after their watch ended, saw a large echo about 130 miles out. They telephoned the information center at Fort Shafter but were told to "Forget it." The attack on Pearl Harbor began at 7:55.

Dec161944
World War II

Holding the Lines in the Bulge

On December 16, 1944, German forces attacked through the Ardennes, opening the Battle of the Bulge. Though American units were caught by surprise, their communications held. Col. Garland C. Black, signal officer of Gen. Omar Bradley's 12th Army Group, had built alternate routes for key circuits, using French underground cables backed by open wire lines. Bradley never lost contact with his armies.

Dec181958
Satellites

SCORE: A Voice from Orbit

On December 18, 1958, the Signal Corps, with an Air Force Atlas rocket, launched Project SCORE, the world's first communications satellite. It showed that an orbiting relay could receive, store, and send on voice and coded signals. SCORE broadcast a taped Christmas greeting from President Eisenhower: "my voice is coming to you from a satellite circling in outer space." It was built to last only weeks.

Dec231907
Army Aviation

The Army Advertises for an Airplane

On December 23, 1907, the Signal Corps issued Specification No. 486, seeking bids for a "heavier-than-air flying machine." It had to carry two people, fly at least 40 miles per hour, and stay aloft for an hour. Bids were due February 1, 1908. The Wright brothers won the contract, and their machine became the Army's first airplane, Signal Corps No. 1.

Also this monthIn December 1936, Signal Corps engineers ran the first field test of their radar at the Newark, New Jersey, airport, where it detected an airplane seven miles away.
January
Moon Echo to CyberJanuary links the Signal Corps' 1946 radar echo from the Moon with Fort Gordon's 2014 start as the Army's Cyber Center of Excellence.
Jan101946
Signal Corps Laboratories

Project Diana Reaches the Moon

On January 10, 1946, a Signal Corps team at the Evans Signal Laboratory in New Jersey bounced a radar signal off the Moon and received its echo about 2.5 seconds later. Led by John H. DeWitt, and drawing on scientist Walter S. McAfee's calculations, the team used a modified SCR-271 radar. Project Diana showed that signals could pass through the ionosphere into space.

All monthJan1871
Signal Service Weather

Cleveland Abbe Joins the Signal Corps

In January 1871 the Signal Corps hired Professor Cleveland Abbe of the Cincinnati Observatory as a weather forecaster. Abbe had issued forecasts in Cincinnati since 1869, and the Corps adopted many of his methods. Though once skeptical of a military weather service, he stayed with it for its full twenty years and founded the Monthly Weather Review.

All monthJan1912
Army Aviation in Augusta

Wilbur Wright Visits Augusta

In January 1912 Wilbur Wright visited the Signal Corps aviation school wintering on the Barnes farm in Augusta, Georgia. His visit came during an unusually harsh winter, with heavy snow in January and February that cut into flying time. The Army's branch history calls him "no doubt a source of much valuable advice." Wright died of typhoid fever that May, at age forty-five.

All monthJan1943
Women in the Signal Corps

WAACs Join the Signal Corps

Beginning in January 1943, the Signal Corps received its first female soldiers, members of the Women's Army Auxiliary Corps, later the Women's Army Corps. At least 5,000 WACs served with the Corps at home and overseas. They worked as message center clerks and switchboard operators, in film libraries and laboratories, and in signal intelligence duties such as cryptography.

Jan242014
Fort Gordon / Cyber

Fort Gordon Becomes the Cyber Center of Excellence

On January 24, 2014, the Army issued an order (EXORD 057-14) establishing the U.S. Army Cyber Center of Excellence and a Cyber School at Fort Gordon. The Signal Center of Excellence became the Cyber Center of Excellence, with the Signal and Cyber Schools under one two-star command. The Army's Cyber Branch followed on September 1, 2014.

Also this monthOn January 4, 2016, the first Cyber Captains Career Course began at the Army Cyber School at Fort Gordon, with twenty officers starting the 26-week course.
February
Weather and WingsIn February 1870 Congress gave the Signal Corps a national weather service, and in February 1908 the Corps signed the Army's first airplane contract.
Feb91870
Weather

Congress Creates a Weather Service

On February 9, 1870, President Ulysses S. Grant signed a joint resolution of Congress directing the Secretary of War to take weather observations at military stations and to warn the Great Lakes and seacoast of approaching storms by telegraph. Chief Signal Officer Albert J. Myer asked for the job, and the Signal Corps took on the work that grew into today's National Weather Service.

Feb41881
Signal Corps founder

Fort Whipple Becomes Fort Myer

On February 4, 1881, the Army renamed Fort Whipple, Virginia, as Fort Myer to honor Albert J. Myer, the Army's first signal officer and founder of the Signal Corps. Signal training had been held at the post. Myer had died the previous August at age 51, soon after his promotion to brigadier general. As head of the weather service, he was popularly known as "Old Probabilities."

Feb101906
Leaders

Greely's Long Watch Ends

On February 10, 1906, President Theodore Roosevelt promoted Chief Signal Officer Adolphus W. Greely to major general, and the War Department sent him to command the Pacific Division at San Francisco. Greely had led the Signal Corps for nearly nineteen years, the longest of any chief signal officer, guiding it from the weather-service years into the electrical age.

Feb101908
Aviation

The Army Orders Its First Airplane

On February 10, 1908, the Signal Corps and Wilbur and Orville Wright signed the first formal Army airplane contract. Under Signal Corps Specification No. 486, the machine had to carry two people at about 40 miles per hour and stay in the air for at least an hour. The price was $25,000; a speed bonus later raised the payment to $30,000.

Feb81968
Fort Gordon

Last Signal OCS Class at Fort Gordon

On February 8, 1968, the 53 members of Class 04-68 took the oath as second lieutenants in Fort Gordon's Alexander Hall, ending the Signal Officer Candidate School. Activated at Fort Gordon in September 1965, the 23-week course had trained more than 2,000 officers. Many graduates soon carried heavy responsibilities in Vietnam, often beyond their rank.

Also this monthIn February 1942 the Signal Corps bought Paramount's film studio in Astoria, Long Island, which opened that May as the Signal Corps Photographic Center.
March
A Corps Takes ShapeMarch marks both the 1863 law that created a Signal Corps for the Civil War and the 1956 order that made Camp Gordon the permanent Fort Gordon.
Mar31863
Civil War

Lincoln Signs the Signal Corps Into Law

On March 3, 1863, President Abraham Lincoln signed a bill that organized a Signal Corps for the duration of the "present rebellion." Until then, Albert J. Myer had depended on officers and men detailed from other units. The law created a chief signal officer with the rank of colonel and required entrance examinations. Historian Rebecca Raines called it "truly a signal victory."

Mar51913
Aviation

From Augusta to the 1st Aero Squadron

In February 1913, trouble with Mexico led the Army to mobilize along the border, and Signal Corps aviators wintering at Augusta, Georgia, were called to Texas. On March 5, 1913, at Texas City, they were organized as the 1st Aero Squadron (Provisional), with nine airplanes, under Capt. Charles deForest Chandler. Augusta's winter flying school later lent its area to Camp Gordon's story.

Mar61918
Women in the Signal Corps

First Women Operators Sail for France

On March 6, 1918, the first unit of 33 Signal Corps women telephone operators sailed for Europe aboard the Army transport Celtic, led by chief operator Grace Banker. Fluent in French and English, they began official duties in France on March 24 and ran American Expeditionary Forces switchboards in Paris, Chaumont, and seventy-five other French cities and towns.

Mar211956
Fort Gordon

Camp Gordon Becomes Fort Gordon

On March 21, 1956, the Army redesignated Camp Gordon, Georgia, as Fort Gordon, making it a permanent installation. Opened as a World War II training camp near Augusta, the post became a Signal Corps training center in 1948, home of the Southeastern Signal School. It grew quickly during the Korean War, when the Army needed many more trained signal soldiers.

Mar241976
Notable soldiers

First Black General of the Signal Corps

On March 24, 1976, Brig. Gen. Emmett Paige Jr. became the Signal Corps' first Black general officer. He had commanded the 361st Signal Battalion in Vietnam and was then leading the 11th Signal Group at Fort Huachuca, Arizona. Paige went on to lead four Army communications and electronics organizations and retired in 1988 after more than 41 years in uniform.

Mar282014
Cyber

Signal Center Becomes Cyber Center

On March 28, 2014, according to the Army Cyber School's chronology, the Signal Center of Excellence at Fort Gordon transitioned to the Cyber Center of Excellence. An Army order that January had created the new center and a Cyber School to stand beside the Signal School. The Army formally established the Cyber Branch on September 1, 2014.

Also this monthOn March 17, 1958, the Navy launched Vanguard I, the first solar-powered satellite, using solar cells developed by the Signal Corps.
April
Keeping Word MovingFrom the San Francisco earthquake of April 1906 to the first weather satellite in April 1960, the Signal Corps kept information flowing when it mattered.
Apr61865
Civil War

The Signal Corps' Civil War Medal of Honor

On April 6, 1865, near Jetersville, Virginia, during Lee's retreat from Petersburg, Pvt. Morgan D. Lane of the Signal Corps helped capture crew members of the Confederate gunboat Nansemond and seized the gunboat's flag. His Medal of Honor, issued in March 1866, was the only one earned by a Civil War Signal Corps soldier. Lee surrendered three days later.

Apr181906
Disaster response

Wires Down in San Francisco

The San Francisco earthquake struck early on April 18, 1906, and fire soon cut the city's last telegraph links to the rest of the country. By about five hours after the quake, Capt. Leonard D. Wildman, the departmental signal officer, had a field telegraph line running from the Presidio to the edge of the fire. Signal Corps operators and equipment then helped commercial companies restore service.

Apr11960
Space and weather

The First Weather Satellite

On April 1, 1960, NASA launched TIROS, the world's first weather satellite. Signal Corps scientists at Fort Monmouth, New Jersey, worked with NASA, the Weather Bureau, RCA, and others on its television camera system, and a ground station at Fort Monmouth received its cloud pictures. TIROS operated for 78 days and showed that satellites could watch the world's weather.

Apr11966
Vietnam

One Brigade for Vietnam's Signals

On April 1, 1966, the Army activated a new signal brigade in Vietnam, soon named the 1st Signal Brigade, to bring nearly all signal units in the country under one command. Led by Robert D. Terry, it was headquartered first at Saigon and later Long Binh. It merged tactical and strategic communications and was the first brigade of its kind in Signal Corps history.

Also this monthOn April 1, 2014, MSG Phillip E. Williams became the first of seven soldiers assigned to work only on building the new Army Cyber School at Fort Gordon.
May
Handing Off, Moving UpIn May 1918 the Signal Corps handed military aviation to the War Department, and in May 2016 Fort Gordon graduated the Army's first class of Cyber officers.
May201918
World War I / Aviation

Aviation Leaves the Signal Corps

On May 20, 1918, the president issued an executive order that fully separated military aviation from the Signal Corps and placed it under the direct control of the Secretary of War. The Division of Military Aeronautics and the Bureau of Aircraft Production became independent agencies within the War Department. The Signal Corps had guided Army aviation through its early years. It kept one part of the work: responsibility for airborne radio.

May11957
Pigeons

The Army's Pigeon Lofts Close

On May 1, 1957, the Signal Corps closed its Pigeon Breeding and Training Branch at Fort Monmouth. Electronics had made the Army's message-carrying birds obsolete, much as progress had earlier retired the Army's horses and mules. The Corps sold its pigeons to the public. Its remaining war heroes, such as G.I. Joe, were presented to zoos around the country instead.

May131968
Vietnam

Attack on Black Virgin Mountain

On the night of May 13, 1968, Viet Cong forces attacked a remote Signal Corps site atop Nui Ba Den, or Black Virgin Mountain, near Tay Ninh in South Vietnam. Twenty-three communicators were killed, and most of their equipment was destroyed. Pfc. Thomas M. Torma of the 86th Signal Battalion received the Silver Star for his heroism while defending the site. It was one of the worst attacks on signal soldiers of the war.

May151984
Information age

Army Information Systems Command

On May 15, 1984, the Army Communications Command became the Army Information Systems Command. The change brought communications, automation, visual information, publications and printing, and records management under one headquarters, an approach the Army called the Information Mission Area. The new command also took in the Army Computer Systems Command. Lt. Gen. Clarence E. McKnight Jr. organized it.

May262016
Cyber / Fort Gordon

First Cyber Officers Graduate

On May 26, 2016, twenty-one officers graduated at Fort Gordon as the first class of the Cyber Basic Officer Leader Course. Their ten months of training had begun on August 6, 2015, less than a year after the Army established its Cyber branch. Col. Jennifer Buckner, the first commandant of the U.S. Army Cyber School, addressed the graduates.

Also this monthIn May 1944 the Signal Corps sent a nine-word test message around the world over the Army Command and Administrative Network (ACAN) in three and one-half minutes; a similar test a year later took nine and a half seconds.
June
Happy Birthday, Signal CorpsThe branch born on June 21, 1860, made Fort Gordon its regimental home on June 1, 1986.
Jun211860
Founding

The Signal Corps Is Born

On June 21, 1860, President James Buchanan signed an Army appropriations bill that added one signal officer to the Army staff. With that act the U.S. Army Signal Corps was born, making the U.S. Army the first in the world with a dedicated communications branch. On June 27 the Senate confirmed Dr. Albert J. Myer, an Army surgeon and inventor of the 'wigwag' flag system, as signal officer with the rank of major.

Jun221884
Arctic / Weather science

Greely Found at Cape Sabine

On June 22, 1884, a rescue expedition under Capt. Winfield Scott Schley found 1st Lt. Adolphus W. Greely of the Signal Corps at Cape Sabine in the Arctic. Greely's party had gone to Lady Franklin Bay in 1881 to make weather and other scientific observations as part of an international polar research effort. Resupply efforts had failed. Only Greely and six others were still alive, and one of them died soon after.

Jun61944
World War II / Combat camera

First Pictures from Omaha Beach

On June 6, 1944, Capt. Herman V. Wall commanded the 165th Signal Photographic Company, First Army's photo unit, as its teams covered the Normandy landings on both American beaches. Wall photographed the assault on Omaha Beach himself and was seriously wounded; he later lost his left leg. He still made sure his film reached England for processing. His were the first pictures received of the actual landings.

Jun11986
Fort Gordon

Fort Gordon Becomes Regimental Home

On June 1, 1986, the Signal Corps Regiment was established under the U.S. Army Regimental System, with Fort Gordon as its regimental home base. The whole branch formed one regiment, and every soldier trained in a Signal specialty joined it on graduating from the branch school. Two days later the commander of the Signal Center and Fort Gordon also became chief of signal. Maj. Gen. Thurman D. Rodgers was the first.

Jun102025
Fort Gordon

A Medal of Honor Namesake

On June 10, 2025, the Army announced that the post would carry the name Fort Gordon in honor of Master Sgt. Gary I. Gordon. He received the Medal of Honor, posthumously, for his valor during the 1993 Battle of Mogadishu, Somalia. The installation had first taken the Gordon name as Camp Gordon at its groundbreaking in October 1941.

July
Eyes Above the BattleFrom a signal station on Little Round Top in July 1863 to a balloon over Santiago in July 1898, the Signal Corps watched the battlefield from above and reported what it saw.
Jul21863
Civil War

Signals from Little Round Top

At Gettysburg, signalmen were the first Union troops to occupy Little Round Top. Just before noon on July 2, 1863, the station warned army headquarters that one mile west, "the woods are full of them." That afternoon Capt. James S. Hall signaled that a heavy Confederate column was moving against the Union left. The column's slow countermarch gave Maj. Gen. George G. Meade time to send troops.

Jul11898
Spanish-American War

A Balloon over San Juan Hill

On July 1, 1898, during the battle for Santiago, Cuba, a Signal Corps balloon under Maj. Joseph Maxfield went up with Lt. Col. George F. Derby aboard. Ordered close to the front, it drew Spanish fire and was torn apart. Still, the observers reported strong Spanish defenses on San Juan Hill and found an unknown trail that sped American troops toward the hill.

Jul11891
Weather

The Weather Service Leaves the Army

On July 1, 1891, the nation's weather service left the Signal Corps. Under a law passed the year before, it moved to the Department of Agriculture as a civilian agency, renamed the Weather Bureau. The Signal Corps had run the service for about two decades. Its enlisted weather observers could leave the Army and join the new bureau, and the Corps shrank to nine officers and fifty sergeants plus the chief signal officer.

Jul181914
Aviation

The Aviation Section Is Created

On July 18, 1914, Congress created the Aviation Section of the Signal Corps, with up to 60 officers and 260 enlisted men. For the first time, the Army had soldiers permanently assigned to aviation duties. The law limited aviation officers to unmarried lieutenants no older than thirty. The section was responsible for operating all military aircraft, including balloons and airplanes.

Jul11974
Fort Gordon

Home of the Signal Corps

On July 1, 1974, as the Army consolidated most of its signal training at Fort Gordon, the post's Southeastern Signal School was renamed the U.S. Army Signal School. The school at Fort Monmouth became the Communications-Electronics School. Three months later, on October 1, Fort Gordon became the U.S. Army Signal Center and Fort Gordon, the new "home of the Signal Corps."

Jul242020
Cyber / Fort Gordon

Army Cyber Command Arrives

On July 24, 2020, U.S. Army Cyber Command uncased its colors at Fortitude Hall, its new headquarters at Fort Gordon. Created in October 2010, the command had been based in the Washington, D.C., area, with elements at Fort Belvoir and Fort Meade. Its commander, Lt. Gen. Stephen G. Fogarty, said the ceremony symbolized "the permanent establishment of ARCYBER headquarters at Fort Gordon."

Also this monthOn July 30, 1909, Orville Wright and 1st Lt. Benjamin D. Foulois flew the Signal Corps' ten-mile speed test between Fort Myer and Alexandria, Virginia, watched by about seven thousand people, including President Taft.
August
Signals Outrun SilenceIn August 1898 American troops fought at Manila because word of a cease-fire arrived too late; in August 1964 the Army opened the first satellite link in a combat zone.
Aug141861
Civil War

A Telegraph Train for the Army

On August 14, 1861, Assistant Secretary of War Thomas A. Scott authorized Albert J. Myer, founder of the Signal Corps, to buy a small telegraph train. The "train" was a set of horse-drawn wagons carrying telegraph sets, reels of insulated wire, and iron lances for stringing temporary "flying telegraph lines." Electric signals were faster than Myer's flags and torches, which averaged about three words per minute.

Aug131898
Spanish-American War

The Battle Fought After the Peace

On August 13, 1898, American troops fought the Battle of Manila, unaware that a cease-fire had been signed the day before. Manila's cable had been cut, so the news arrived days late. Signal detachments served with each division and brigade commander, ran insulated wire along the beach as troops advanced, and used flags to direct Navy gunfire against Spanish positions.

Aug151935
Alaska / Notable soldier

News from the Top of the World

On August 15, 1935, humorist Will Rogers and aviator Wiley Post died when their plane crashed about fifteen miles south of Point Barrow, Alaska. Sgt. Stanley R. Morgan, the Signal Corps radio operator at Barrow, learned of the crash from a Native runner and went to the scene. Returning to his station, he signaled news of the tragedy to the world.

All monthAug1964
Satellites / Vietnam

First Satellite Link in a Combat Zone

In August 1964, after the Tonkin Gulf incidents exposed weak radio links between Saigon and Washington, the Army rushed an experimental SYNCOM satellite ground terminal to Vietnam. Run by a team under Warrant Officer Jack H. Inman, it went into service late that month, giving one telephone and one teletype circuit to Hawaii. It was the first use of satellite communications in a combat zone.

Aug42014
Cyber / Fort Gordon

The Army Cyber School Opens

On August 4, 2014, the U.S. Army Cyber School headquarters was unveiled at Fort Gordon by Maj. Gen. LaWarren Patterson, commander of the Cyber Center of Excellence, and Col. Jennifer Buckner was announced as its first commandant. A year later, on August 6, 2015, 31 newly commissioned lieutenants began the first Cyber Basic Officer Leader Course at the school.

Also this monthAlbert J. Myer, founder of the Signal Corps and builder of its weather service, died on August 24, 1880, at age 51.
September
Flags to FirewallsIn September 1862 signalmen on Elk Mountain flagged warnings across the Antietam battlefield; in September 2014 the Army created its Cyber branch to fight in a new domain.
Sep171862
Civil War

Warning from Elk Mountain

At the Battle of Antietam on September 17, 1862, a Union signal station on Elk Mountain overlooked the battlefield. The party under Lt. Joseph Gloskoski spotted A. P. Hill's Confederate division arriving from Harpers Ferry and flagged a warning to General Burnside to watch his left. Burnside was away from his signal station when the message came, and Hill's counterattack drove his troops back.

Sep261884
Notable soldier / Weather

W. Hallett Greene Joins the Signal Corps

On September 26, 1884, W. Hallett Greene became the first Black soldier admitted to the Signal Corps. A new graduate of the College of the City of New York, he had been rejected by Chief Signal Officer William B. Hazen. Secretary of War Robert T. Lincoln overruled Hazen and ordered him accepted. After training, Greene served as a weather observer and led the Signal Corps station at Pensacola, Florida.

Sep121918
World War I

Signal Troops at St. Mihiel

On September 12, 1918, the new U.S. First Army attacked the St. Mihiel salient in France. Signal troops worked to keep units connected. Men of the 55th Telegraph and 317th Field Signal Battalions dug a cable trench six feet deep and one kilometer long through nearly solid rock, without explosives. Six women telephone operators served at First Army headquarters. By September 16 the salient was cleared.

Sep201965
Fort Gordon / Vietnam era

Signal Officer Candidate School at Fort Gordon

On September 20, 1965, an order activated the Signal Officer Candidate School at Fort Gordon, creating the 1st Officer Candidate Battalion under Maj. John J. McManus. The program began with ten officers and no facilities of its own. The first class arrived that November. When the last class was commissioned in February 1968, the school had produced 2,210 second lieutenants during the Vietnam War era.

Sep12014
Cyber

The Cyber Branch Is Born

On September 1, 2014, the Army's Cyber branch was officially established. Secretary of the Army John McHugh had signed General Orders 2014-63 on August 21, and it took effect on the first of September, weeks after the Army Cyber School headquarters was unveiled at Fort Gordon. In 2016 the U.S. Army Center of Military History designated September 1 as the Cyber Corps birthday.

Sep32020
Cyber / Fort Gordon

Army Cyber Command Dedicates Fortitude Hall

On September 3, 2020, U.S. Army Cyber Command dedicated Fortitude Hall, its new headquarters at Fort Gordon. Lt. Gen. Stephen G. Fogarty, Command Sgt. Maj. Sheryl D. Lyon, and deputy Ron Pontius unveiled the dedication plaque. The move brought the command's headquarters from the Washington, D.C., area to Georgia. Staff had begun working in the 336,000-square-foot building in July 2020.

Also this monthOn September 1, 1905, the Signal School opened at Fort Leavenworth, Kansas, recognizing the Signal Corps as a distinct branch of the Army.

How a Civil War signal detail became the Army's nerve system — and how Fort Gordon became its home.

From flags to fiber

The U.S. Army Signal Corps was established on June 21, 1860, when Congress created the position of Signal Officer and Major Albert J. Myer began organizing a dedicated communications service for the Army. Myer's wig-wag flag signaling, developed in the years before the Civil War, gave field commanders something they had never reliably had: the ability to pass orders and observations across a battlefield faster than a rider could carry them.

Over the following century the Corps carried that mission through every generation of technology it touched — telegraph and heliograph in the Indian Wars, and radio experiments in the 1900s and 1910s, battlefield telephone and teletype in two World Wars, satellite and microwave systems in the Cold War, and today's data networks and cyber operations. Each generation of Signaleers built on the last.

Fort Gordon's own role in that story began after the Second World War. Camp Gordon, built in 1941 as a division training post, was nearly deactivated when the war ended — but in October and November 1948 the Army activated the Signal Corps Training Center there. The post was made permanent and renamed Fort Gordon in March 1956, and in June 1986 the Army established the U.S. Army Signal Corps Regiment with Fort Gordon as its regimental home. It has trained Signal Soldiers ever since.

Camp Gordon: The WWI Era

Before Augusta, there was an earlier Camp Gordon near Atlanta, built in 1917 to turn draftees into the 82d "All-American" Division.

A cantonment built in a summer

Camp Gordon was named for Lt. Gen. John B. Gordon, a Georgia native who also served as the state's governor. Construction began on June 18, 1917, about 14 miles from Atlanta in DeKalb County, and the Army established the camp on July 18 as the training camp for the 82d Division of the National Army. The first drafted men reported September 1–15, 1917. The 82d occupied the cantonment from August 1917 to April 1918.

As the war went on, the camp became an infantry replacement and training camp (April 1918), an infantry training center (August 21, 1918), and finally a demobilization center (December 3, 1918), processing about 116,228 soldiers home. Its Infantry Central Officers' Training School ran from June 1918 to February 1919. The buildings were sold in 1920, and the camp was abandoned in September 1921.

The name came back in 1941, when the Army built a new Camp Gordon outside Augusta. That post became Fort Gordon, the home of the Signal Corps. It carried the Gordon name for more than eighty years, until 27 October 2023, when the Army redesignated it Fort Eisenhower for General of the Army Dwight D. Eisenhower — Supreme Allied Commander in Europe and later President. The museum keeps the older name wherever the record uses it, because that is the name under which most of this history was made.

1892–1907

Before the airplane

1892–1902

Balloons come first

Army aviation began with balloons. The Signal Corps set up a balloon section in 1892. In 1898 one balloon, an old silk envelope made at Fort Logan, went with the Army to Cuba, with gas enough for only one inflation. Joseph Maxfield commanded the detachment of three officers and 24 enlisted men. On July 1 Lt. Col. George Derby, the Army commander's engineer officer, went up with him near El Pozo and ordered the balloon closer to the fighting over Maxfield's objection. Its ropes tangled in the brush, and it drew fire onto the troops below. From the basket Derby found a trail that helped speed troops toward San Juan Hill. The Signal Corps formed a new balloon detachment at Fort Myer, Virginia, in 1902.

1898–1907

From Langley to the Wrights

Before the Army bought a Wright airplane, it paid for a different one. In late 1898 the Board of Ordnance and Fortification gave Samuel Langley $50,000, in two allotments of $25,000, to build a full-size flying machine, and made the Chief Signal Officer responsible for the money. Both of his 1903 attempts crashed into the Potomac River, and the board refused him more. Maj. George O. Squier, assistant to the Chief Signal Officer, followed the Wright brothers' work closely and later headed the board that supervised their Army trials.

1907

August 1, 1907: a division of three

On August 1, 1907, the Chief Signal Officer, Brig. Gen. James Allen, created an Aeronautical Division in the Signal Corps to handle “all matters pertaining to military ballooning, air machines, and all kindred subjects.” It started with three people: Capt. Charles deForest Chandler, Cpl. Edward Ward and Pvt. Joseph E. Barrett. Barrett deserted soon afterward, which left Ward as the first enlisted airman. On December 23, 1907, the Army issued Specification No. 486 and asked for bids on a “heavier-than-air flying machine”: it had to carry two people, stay up an hour, carry fuel for 125 miles and average 40 mph.

1908

Fort Myer: the trials begin

1908

Fort Myer: the trials begin

In February 1908 the Army signed a contract with the Wright brothers, and in August the Wrights brought their airplane to Fort Myer. Orville Wright made the first official demonstration on September 3. On September 9 he stayed up for more than an hour, and Lt. Frank Lahm became the first Army officer to fly in an airplane, as a passenger. Then, on September 17, a propeller split in the air, and the plane fell from about 75 feet. Orville was badly hurt and needed about six weeks to recover. His passenger, Lt. Thomas Selfridge, was killed. He was the first person to die in a powered airplane crash.

SC-1 — the Signal Corps' first dirigible, flown at Fort Myer in August 1908. Built by Thomas Scott Baldwin, later "Father of the American Dirigible," with engines by Glenn Curtiss.

Baldwin volunteered for military service again in 1917 at nearly 63 years old, was commissioned a captain in the Signal Corps' Aviation Section, and became Chief of Army Balloon Inspection and Production.

1882–1908

Lt. Thomas Selfridge

Thomas Etholen Selfridge, born in 1882, graduated from West Point in 1903, in the same class as Douglas MacArthur. He became an Army observer on Alexander Graham Bell's flying experiments. On December 6, 1907, he went up to 168 feet in one of Bell's kites. In 1908 he was one of three Army officers trained to fly Army Dirigible No. 1, and he argued that the Army should buy airplanes. He died at age 26 at Fort Myer on September 17, 1908, flying as Orville Wright's passenger. He is buried at Arlington National Cemetery.

Lt. Frank Purdy Lahm
Balloon champion and early Army pilot

Lahm was the first Signal Corps officer certified to fly balloons, dirigibles, and the Wright Flyer alike — and a rare survivor of a Wright Flyer crash. A mannequin honoring him stands today at the National Museum of the U.S. Air Force in Dayton, Ohio, beside a full-scale Wright Flyer replica, among a company of other early Signal Corps aviators.

1909

Signal Corps Airplane No. 1

1909

The Army buys its first airplane

In June 1909 the Wrights returned to Fort Myer with a new airplane, and after a month of practice flights the official tests began on July 27. On July 27 Orville flew a trial of 1 hour, 12 minutes and 40 seconds with Lahm as observer. On July 30 Lt. Benjamin Foulois flew as observer in the speed test, which averaged 42.5 mph. That beat the Army's 40 mph requirement, so the Army paid a $5,000 bonus on top of the price. On August 2, 1909, it accepted the airplane for $30,000 and called it Signal Corps Airplane No. 1. For almost two years it was the Army's only airplane. The aircraft was a small canard biplane with a four-cylinder Wright engine of about 30 horsepower, and a top speed of about 42 mph.

1909

Lt. Frederic Humphreys: the first Army pilot to solo

After the Army accepted the Wright airplane, training began at College Park, Maryland, in October 1909. Wilbur Wright taught two officers, Lt. Frederic Humphreys of the Corps of Engineers and Lt. Frank Lahm. Humphreys was chosen as Foulois's substitute, and on October 26 he became the first Army officer to fly solo. Lahm followed minutes later. On November 5 the two crashed together and both walked away unhurt. Humphreys left the Army in 1910 but stayed in the National Guard, was called up for the Mexican border in 1916 and World War I, and retired as a brigadier general in 1939.

Maj. Gen. Benjamin Delahauf Foulois
First Army pilot on flight status · Official biography ↗

Foulois enlisted in 1898 and, by 1909, was one of the first Army officers to fly in the military's first airplane, purchased from the Wright brothers. On its final trial flight he rode beside Orville Wright as official observer and navigator on a ten-mile cross-country run that averaged more than 42 miles per hour. Posted alone to Fort Sam Houston, Texas, in 1910, he served for a stretch as the Army's only pilot, navigator, instructor, and commander of heavier-than-air aviation — teaching himself to fly by mail correspondence with the Wrights. He made his first solo flight at Fort Sam Houston on March 2, 1910. “I made my first solo, my first landing and my first crackup - all the same day,” he said later. In 1911 he built and flew the first radio receiver ever carried aloft in a U.S. Army aircraft. He organized and commanded the 1st Aero Squadron, led it into Mexico during the 1916 Punitive Expedition under Gen. John J. Pershing, and by 1931 became Chief of the Air Corps — the first military aviator to hold that post.

A career of firsts

  • 1908: first flight as a dirigible pilot
  • 1909: first observer on a cross-country airplane flight
  • 1910: first military man to teach himself to fly; only military test pilot on Old No. 1
  • 1911: first to fly more than 100 miles non-stop; first operational reconnaissance flight; first test of radio in flight
  • 1914: first commander of a tactical air unit, the 1st Aero Squadron
  • 1916: first use of an aircraft in a combat operation (Mexico)
  • 1918: first Chief of Air Service, AEF, First Army
  • 1933: first Air Corps chief awarded the Mackay Trophy
  • 1963: National Aviation Hall of Fame

Decorations include the Army Distinguished Service Medal, the French Legion of Honor (Commander), and the Crown of Italy (Grand Officer).

1909–1912

Learning to fly

1907–1920

Capt. Charles deForest Chandler

Charles Chandler was a veteran Signal Corps officer and balloonist whom Gen. Allen put in charge of the new Aeronautical Division in 1907. He later returned for a second tour as its head and learned to fly from Hap Arnold. In July 1912 he ranked second of the five officers qualified as military aviators. He retired as a colonel in 1920 and kept ballooning as a civilian. In 1943 he and Frank Lahm wrote a history of early Army flying, How Our Army Grew Wings.

1887–1960

Lt. Thomas DeWitt Milling

Thomas Milling was born in Winfield, Louisiana, in 1887 and graduated from West Point in 1909 as a cavalry second lieutenant. He learned to fly with the Wright brothers and received FAI pilot certificate No. 30 on July 6, 1911. He studied at Signal Corps aviation schools in Augusta, Georgia; Texas City, Texas; and San Diego, California. In World War I he helped run Air Service training in Europe and served as chief of staff of the First Army's Air Service under Billy Mitchell. He retired in 1933, returned to duty in World War II, and died in 1960. He was advanced on the retired list to brigadier general.

Col. Roy C. Kirtland
Signal Corps Aviation School, College Park

Kirtland transferred from the Infantry to the Signal Corps' Aviation Section in 1911 and took charge of the Army's aviation school at College Park, Maryland, where he recommended a young officer named Henry H. "Hap" Arnold for flight training — Arnold later became Chief of the Army Air Corps. Kirtland went on to command the 1st Aero Squadron, serve as an aviation inspector overseas in World War I, and retire in 1938 after 40 years of service — only to return to active duty at age 65. Albuquerque Army Air Base was renamed Kirtland Field in his honor in 1942, at Hap Arnold's personal request; it is known today as Kirtland Air Force Base.

Capt. Henry H. "Hap" Arnold
Signal Corps aviator

Arnold learned to fly in 1911 on the recommendation of Roy Kirtland, then in charge of the Army's aviation school at College Park. He is shown here as a captain in the Signal Corps. Arnold went on to lead the Army Air Forces through the Second World War, and in 1942 he personally asked that Albuquerque's air base be renamed for his lifelong friend Kirtland.

1911–1917

Lt. Henry “Hap” Arnold, the young aviator

Hap Arnold was an infantry second lieutenant when he arrived at College Park in June 1911. He trained with the Wright brothers in Dayton for 11 days before flying solo. In July 1912 he ranked first among the Army's qualified military aviators. According to one account he introduced flying goggles after a bug flew into his eye while landing. He won the MacKay Trophy twice. He served at San Diego, where he had to overcome a fear of flying left by an accident at Fort Riley. He became a colonel in August 1917 and executive officer of the Army's Aeronautical Division. In 1944 he was promoted to General of the Army, and in 1949 he became the first five-star General of the Air Force.

1911

Signal Corps Airplane No. 3

With its first $25,000 for aviation, the Signal Corps ordered five new airplanes in 1911: three of the Wright Type B design and two built by Glenn Curtiss. Signal Corps Airplane No. 3, a Wright Type B with wheels attached to its skids, was accepted at Fort Sam Houston on April 27, 1911. It moved to College Park that June and went to Augusta with the school for the winter of 1912–13.

1911

Fort Sam Houston, 1911: Beck and Kelly

In 1911 the Army's flying detachment at Fort Sam Houston, Texas, had two new airplanes. Lt. Paul Beck, the senior pilot by rank, took command of the Provisional Aero Company in April. On May 10, Lt. George E. M. Kelly was killed landing the Curtiss airplane, S.C. No. 2. Foulois believed poor maintenance played a part, but an investigating board disagreed. Beck also trained at the Curtiss school on North Island in San Diego. He later returned to the infantry and died in 1922.

1912

The first military aviators

On February 23, 1912, the War Department announced the rating of Military Aviator. The first certificates went to Capt. Charles Chandler and Lts. Hap Arnold and Thomas Milling, who all qualified on July 5, 1912. Capt. Paul Beck followed on July 12 and Lt. Benjamin Foulois on July 13. Twenty-four officers are counted as the original Military Aviators under the 1913 order that authorized their badge.

1911–1913 · Augusta, Georgia

The winter flying school

1911–1912

The first winter in Augusta

Winter weather made flying at College Park, Maryland, hard, so the Signal Corps sent its aviation school south. On November 28, 1911, five officers, 20 enlisted men and four airplanes left College Park by special train for Augusta. Capt. Charles Chandler commanded, with Lts. Hap Arnold, Roy Kirtland and Thomas Milling as pilots. The airplanes sat in canvas tent hangars on the Barnes farm, leased for $15 a month, near Sand Bar Ferry Road. Flying began on December 7. Heavy snow stopped flying in January and again in February 1912, and then the Savannah River flooded the field. The soldiers had set the airplanes on platforms, and none was damaged. Wilbur Wright visited on January 20. On January 25 Arnold set an Army altitude record of 4,764 feet. The school flew on only 58 of its 124 days in Augusta and closed on April 1, 1912.

1912–1913

The second winter, and the road back to Augusta

The school came back on a nine-car special train that left College Park on November 18, 1912, carrying five airplanes. Chandler again commanded. At Augusta a Wright Company pilot flew a new Wright speed scout at 66.9 miles per hour. On February 25, 1913, Chandler flew 28 miles south to Waynesboro, Georgia, and came back the next morning in 36 minutes. On February 28, 1913, five officers, 21 enlisted men and their airplanes left on an eleven-car train for Texas City, Texas, because of trouble with Mexico. There the school began operating as the 1st Aero Squadron, the Army's first flying unit. The Signal Corps came back to the Augusta area in November 1948, when it opened its training center at Camp Gordon.

1912–1917

North Island and the Aviation Section

1912–1916

North Island and the Aviation Section

In December 1912 the Signal Corps aviation school opened on North Island in San Diego, a site that became Rockwell Field. There the Army's first aero squadron flew Burgess and Curtiss airplanes. On July 18, 1914, Congress created an Aviation Section in the Signal Corps. It replaced the Aeronautical Division as the Army's aviation authority, and the old division became its Washington office. In 1916 the 1st Aero Squadron went into Mexico with the Punitive Expedition, which the San Diego Air & Space Museum calls the Army's first aviation combat experience. The squadron flew Curtiss JN-3 airplanes.

Grover Loening's fix

When Congress wouldn't fund new aircraft, the Signal Corps' chief engineer at North Island built his own.

A composite trainer built by Grover Loening from salvaged Burgess, Curtiss, and Martin parts, circa 1914 — 200 pounds lighter and roughly 70 mph faster than the aircraft it replaced.

As the Signal Corps' chief engineer at San Diego's North Island, Loening was frustrated by Congress's reluctance to fund new aircraft for a worn-out fleet. Rather than wait, he combined a Burgess-built trainer, a Curtiss airplane, and salvaged parts — possibly from a Martin trainer — into a single lighter, faster machine, building his own tail section and landing gear from scratch. He later helped shape a generation of aircraft designers, including Leroy Grumman and his co-founders, who worked under him before founding the company that became Northrop Grumman.

1914–1917

Trouble in the air

The new Aviation Section struggled. Inspectors found little progress at the San Diego school in January 1915. A board in 1915–16 found that flight pay had gone to staff who were not pilots and that some aircraft the Army accepted were substandard. In May 1916 the Secretary of War removed the section's chief and briefly put Capt. William “Billy” Mitchell in his place. Lt. Col. George Squier was chosen to lead the section, and in February 1917 he became Chief Signal Officer. When the United States entered the war in April 1917, the Aviation Section had 52 officers, 1,100 enlisted men, 210 civilians and 55 airplanes. All of them were training models.

1917–1918

Leaving the Signal Corps

1917–1918

Leaving the Signal Corps

The war changed everything. In July 1917 Congress appropriated $640 million for aircraft. Gen. Pershing created a separate Air Service for the American Expeditionary Forces in June 1917, so aviation overseas was never a Signal Corps job. The Army adopted the Curtiss JN-4 “Jenny” as its training airplane, and a commission sent to Europe recommended European designs such as the DH-4 for combat. Over 15,000 Liberty engines were built, but fewer than 1,000 American-built planes saw action, and pilots mostly flew French machines. On May 20, 1918, an executive order took aviation out of the Signal Corps completely; it kept only airborne radio. The Army had begun with one airplane in 1907 and received nearly 17,000 during the war.

1917–1918 · France

Signal Corps wings over the Western Front

The Army went to war in April 1917 with an air arm that belonged to the Signal Corps. Its Aviation Section had 52 officers, about 1,100 enlisted men and 55 airplanes, and every one of those airplanes was a trainer. At home, the Signal Corps raised the new aero squadrons, ordered thousands of Curtiss JN-4 trainers and sent its cadets to flying school.

Overseas the arrangement was different. In June 1917 Gen. John J. Pershing created a separate Air Service for the American Expeditionary Forces, so the squadrons the Signal Corps organized fought in France under the Air Service, and mostly in French-built airplanes. On May 20, 1918, an executive order took aviation out of the Signal Corps entirely. The Corps kept one piece of it: radio for airplanes.

This gallery follows those squadrons from the training fields to the front: the airplanes, the insignia painted on their sides, the airfields they flew from, and the kinds of missions they flew.

Curtiss JN-4D “Jenny”

Role trainerEngine Curtiss OX-5, 90–100 hpTop speed 75 mph

The airplane nearly every American pilot of the war learned on. After April 1917 the Signal Corps ordered Jennies in large numbers, and by the time production ended after the Armistice more than 6,000 had been delivered, most of them JN-4Ds. The Air Force estimates that about 95 percent of trainees flew one. After the war the Army sold hundreds as surplus, and the Jenny became the barnstormer's airplane of the 1920s.

Drawn as Signal Corps No. 4037, the Love Field, Texas, Jenny of 1918 restored by the National Museum of the U.S. Air Force.

Nieuport 28

Role pursuit (fighter)Engine Gnome rotary, 160 hpCrew 1

The first fighter of the American pursuit squadrons. The 94th and 95th Aero Squadrons received Nieuport 28s in early 1918, at first without machine guns. On April 14, 1918, Lts. Alan Winslow and Douglas Campbell of the 94th took off from Gengoult Aerodrome near Toul and shot down two German airplanes over their own field while the people of Toul watched. The Air Service judged the Nieuport 28 unsuitable for front-line work and replaced it with the SPAD XIII that summer.

The Air Service's main fighter. Built in France, it was about 10 mph faster than the newest German fighters. The Air Service accepted 893 SPAD XIIIs, and they equipped 15 of the 16 American fighter squadrons. Eddie Rickenbacker of the 94th and Frank Luke of the 27th, the “Arizona Balloon Buster,” both flew it.

Salmson 2A2

Role observationEngine Salmson radialCrew pilot & observer

A French two-seater built for the job the Army valued most from its airmen: seeing the enemy. The 1st and 12th Aero Squadrons began flying Salmsons over the front in the summer of 1918, and one history of the American air war rates it the best observation airplane the Air Service had. The pilot flew; the observer behind him watched, photographed, and defended the airplane with a ring-mounted machine gun.

Drawn in the markings of the 1st Aero Squadron, whose emblem was the American flag.

De Havilland DH-4 “Liberty Plane”

Role observation & day bombingEngine Liberty V-12, 400 hpBuilt 4,846 in the U.S.

The only American-built airplane to see combat in the war, a British design re-engineered around the American Liberty engine. Dayton-Wright, Fisher Body and Standard Aircraft built 4,846 by the Armistice. The first reached France on May 11, 1918, and the 135th Aero Squadron, nicknamed the “Liberty Squadron,” flew its first combat mission in early August. A hit could set it ablaze, and crews called it the “flaming coffin.” Four of the six Medals of Honor awarded to American aviators in the war went to DH-4 crews.

Drawn in the olive drab of an Air Service observation squadron, with the 1918 national insignia.

The 1918 national insignia

General Order 299 of February 8, 1918, gave AEF airplanes a roundel of red outside, blue, and a white center, painted on top of the upper wing, and rudder stripes with the red next to the hinge. The order reversed the French colors so American airplanes could be told apart. It replaced the white star the Army had used since 1917.

The emblems the squadrons chose

Painted on the fuselage

In 1918 each aero squadron picked its own emblem and painted it on the sides of its airplanes. Several are still flown by the squadrons that carry their lineage today. Shown here as simplified museum renditions.

The flag
1st Aero Squadron

The Army's oldest flying unit, which flew for Pershing in Mexico in 1916, reached Europe on September 1, 1917. Its emblem was the American flag, painted by hand. The Air Force Museum holds the first aircraft insignia taken from an Air Service airplane: a painted flag cut from an observation plane the French gave the AEF at Amanty in February 1918.

Hat in the Ring
94th Aero Squadron

Squadron commander Maj. John Huffer proposed Uncle Sam's stovepipe hat, and the flight surgeon, Paul Walters, added the ring: America had thrown its hat into the ring. Squadron custom kept the brim pointed up so the luck would not run out. The National Air and Space Museum holds one cut from the SPAD of ace H. Weir Cook.

Kicking Mule
95th Aero Squadron

The 95th took its emblem from its mascot, a mule named Jake. With the 94th it formed the 1st Pursuit Group on May 5, 1918, the first American fighter group.

The Eagle
27th Aero Squadron

The 27th's pilots settled on an eagle with outspread wings, drawn after a company's eagle trademark of the day. Its best-known pilot, Lt. Frank Luke Jr., became famous for attacking German observation balloons.

Who said Rats!
147th Aero Squadron

The fourth squadron of the 1st Pursuit Group painted a black terrier on its SPADs over the words “Who said Rats!”

Red Devil
96th Aero Squadron

A red devil thumbing his nose and holding a white bomb, inside a black triangle edged in white. On June 12, 1918, flying French Breguet bombers from Amanty, the 96th made the first American daylight bombing raid of the war, against the rail yards at Dommary-Baroncourt.

Eyes, guns and bombs

Three kinds of mission

The Army saw the airplane first as an observer, and in 1918 observation remained the job it valued most. Pursuit squadrons fought to keep the sky open for the observers, and bombing squadrons struck behind the lines. Weather permitting, squadrons flew patrols at dawn and at dusk every day, and more missions as the fighting called for them.

Observation

Two-seaters carried a pilot and an observer, both officers; a corps observation squadron had 18 of each. They photographed enemy positions, watched the roads, flew contact patrols over the infantry, and adjusted artillery fire. Troops below answered with cloth panels laid out on the ground in agreed patterns. Radio for the airplanes stayed a Signal Corps responsibility even after aviation left the Corps.

Pursuit

Single-seat fighters flew in formation, patrolled their sector of the front, escorted the observers and bombers, and attacked enemy airplanes and the tethered balloons the Germans used to watch American lines. Squadrons were grouped by role; the 1st Pursuit Group, formed at Toul in May 1918, was the first American fighter group.

Day bombardment

Two-seat bombers flew in formation against rail yards, roads and troop concentrations behind the German lines, with fighters as escort. On October 9, 1918, Col. Billy Mitchell sent a large force of bombers and escorting fighters against German troops gathering in the Meuse-Argonne.

Gen. Pershing later said that no army had ever gone into battle knowing as much about what lay in front of it as his did at St. Mihiel and the Argonne.

Their names on the map

Airfields named for Signal Corps aviators

Several major U.S. installations still carry the names of Signal Corps pilots.

Selfridge Field

Selfridge's name lives on in Michigan. In 1917 the Army leased and then bought Joy Aviation Field in Harrison Township and named it Selfridge Field. It first trained pilots before the United States entered World War I. It became Selfridge Air Force Base in 1947 and Selfridge Air National Guard Base in 1971.

Kelly Field

Lt. George E. M. Kelly was killed in a crash at Fort Sam Houston, Texas, on May 10, 1911. In 1917 a new flying field south of San Antonio was named Kelly Field in his honor. Benjamin Foulois had chosen the site in 1916. It opened as an aviation camp on March 27, 1917, and flying began on April 5.

Clark Air Base, Philippines
America's first air base west of Hawaii

Named for Maj. Harold M. Clark of the U.S. Army Signal Corps, the first American to fly in Hawaii. Clark died on May 2, 1919, in a seaplane crash in Panama and is buried at Arlington National Cemetery.

Kirtland Air Force Base, New Mexico — named for Col. Roy C. Kirtland (profiled above). Today it is the third-largest base in Air Force Materiel Command.

Wheeler Army Airfield, Hawaii
2d Lt. Sheldon H. Wheeler, Sept. 22, 1915

The Oahu airfield is named for Sheldon H. Wheeler, photographed here as a young Signal Corps aviator beside his biplane.

MacDill Air Force Base, Florida
2d Lt. Leslie MacDill, Sept. 1, 1914

The Tampa base carries the name of Leslie MacDill, another Signal Corps aviator of the pre-WWI aviation section. General Dynamics, which grew out of the Consolidated and Convair companies founded by fellow Signal Corps veteran Reuben Fleet, keeps offices there today.

After the Corps

The Signal Corps' aviators built the defense industry

A striking thread runs from the Signal Corps' pre-WWI aviation section into nearly every major American aircraft manufacturer.

Jack Northrop was drafted into the infantry in 1917, but the Army discovered he had been a draftsman for the Loughead brothers — later Lockheed — and released him early to design aircraft instead. He founded Northrop Corporation in 1939; it merged with Grumman in 1994. Grumman's own founder, Leroy Grumman, and his partners had worked for Grover Cleveland Loening, the Signal Corps' civilian chief engineer at San Diego, who had himself managed the Wright brothers' factory in Dayton.

Lawrence "Larry" Bell worked for Glenn L. Martin's aircraft company before being hired by fellow Signal Corps aviator Maj. Reuben Fleet at Consolidated Aircraft, which Fleet founded in Buffalo in 1923 and moved to San Diego in 1935. Bell later founded Bell Aircraft, builder of the Bell X-1 that broke the sound barrier with Chuck Yeager at the controls, and of the UH-1 Huey helicopter of Vietnam War fame.

Fleet's Consolidated built the PBY Catalina flying boat, which located the German battleship Bismarck in 1941, and the B-24 Liberator bomber. After merging with Vultee Aircraft, the company became Convair — builder of the six-engine, four-jet B-36 Peacemaker — before General Dynamics acquired it in 1953.

Glenn L. Martin's company was itself a training ground: Donald Douglas left to found Douglas Aircraft (builder of the DC-3 and the SBD Dive Bomber that sank four Japanese carriers at Midway); James McDonnell founded McDonnell Aircraft; and James "Dutch" Kindelberger founded North American Aviation, builder of the P-51 Mustang and the B-25 Mitchell flown in the Doolittle Raid. Douglas, McDonnell, and Boeing would all eventually merge into one company by 1997.

Elsewhere in the industry: Chance Vought's company built the F4U Corsair and the F-8 Crusader before a chain of mergers — LTV, Northrop Grumman, and finally Triumph Group. Bendix built the twin machine-gun turret for the B-17's nose before being absorbed into Allied Corporation and then Honeywell. And the light-aircraft makers Beechcraft, Cessna, and Lycoming — the last of which powered the Bell UH-1 Huey — are all part of Textron today. Stinson, maker of the scout planes that flew artillery-spotting missions for the Army Air Forces in WWII, was bought by Convair in 1948, which General Dynamics acquired in 1953.

It's an open question in the collection whether today's major defense contractors would exist in their current form at all, if the Signal Corps hadn't trained the aviators who went on to found them.

Wing Four

The Hello Girls

223 women. One war. A story nearly lost.

In 1918, the U.S. Army Signal Corps sent 223 women to France as telephone operators to help win the First World War. Fluent in French and English and trained on the Army's own switchboards, they connected the calls that moved orders along the front — some working within range of artillery fire. Told through letters, photographs, rare archival footage, and interviews with family and historians, their story remained almost entirely unknown for a century.

Wing Five

Combat Camera & Video

For more than a century, the Signal Corps has been the Army's camera. It documented the fight, trained the troops, and brought some of Hollywood's biggest names into uniform to do it.

The Army's eye

Photography became a Signal Corps mission in the 1880s, and the Corps has held it ever since. Signal Corps photographers covered the American Expeditionary Forces in France in the First World War. In the Second, the Corps opened its own film studio, the Signal Corps Photographic Center in Astoria, New York, and sent photographic detachments to every theater of the war.

Film was a training tool as much as a record. The Signal Corps Pictorial Service made hundreds of training films, and it recruited the people who knew the craft best: directors, writers, animators, and studio executives. Two of them are profiled below from the museum's own fact sheets. Today that lineage lives on in the Army's combat camera Soldiers, who still document operations as they happen.

Hollywood in uniform

Two storytellers who served in the Signal Corps during World War II.

Lt. Col. Theodor Seuss "Ted" Geisel
Dr. Seuss · Signal Corps Pictorial Service

Born March 2, 1904, in Massachusetts, Geisel was a children's author, political cartoonist, illustrator, poet, animator, screenwriter, and filmmaker. He wrote most of his books under the pen name Dr. Seuss, and The Cat in the Hat is the best known of them.

In World War II he served with the Signal Corps Pictorial Service making training films, alongside director Frank Capra (Mr. Smith Goes to Washington, It's a Wonderful Life), animation directors Chuck Jones and Friz Freleng, voice artist Mel Blanc, and music director Carl Stalling. Geisel spoke out against racism at home toward Jews and Black Americans, which he saw as harming the war effort, and some of his early wartime cartoons show it.

Capra came up with Private Snafu, one of the most successful animated characters of the war. Geisel led the animation section and wrote most of the 26 episodes produced between 1943 and 1945. Snafu was a Soldier who usually did the wrong thing out of laziness or ignorance, and troops were meant to learn from his mistakes. The cartoons managed to be funny and instructive at the same time.

Geisel left the Army as a lieutenant colonel and received the Legion of Merit for his wartime service. He died on September 24, 1991, in La Jolla, California. His birthday, March 2, is now National Read Across America Day.

Col. Darryl F. Zanuck
Producer, The Longest Day · Signal Corps

Zanuck was born in Nebraska in 1902 and was working in films by age eight. At fifteen he lied about his age to join the Nebraska National Guard and served on the Mexican border and in France during World War I. At Warner Brothers he wrote Rin Tin Tin pictures, helped the studio switch to sound with The Jazz Singer (1927), and became chief of production, turning out hits like Little Caesar and The Public Enemy. He co-founded Twentieth Century Pictures in 1933, which merged with Fox in 1935 to form Twentieth Century Fox. There he produced The Grapes of Wrath and How Green Was My Valley.

The Army commissioned him a colonel in the Signal Corps in 1942. As a military observer in London, he negotiated agreements with the British Ministry of Information and the Soviet Film Bureau to coordinate information and training films among the Allies. On August 19, 1942, he went along on the Allied raid at Dieppe. He then supervised Report from the Aleutians, the first film in a War Department historical series, which won a New York Film Critics award.

For the next film in the series, At the Front in North Africa, Zanuck organized special photographic detachments to cover the amphibious landings. Nothing in it was staged, and every combat scene was real. The work earned him the Legion of Merit. He supervised filming in Italy before returning to the studio in 1944.

In 1962 he released The Longest Day, the D-Day epic many consider his greatest film. It was shot in Corsica and Normandy with forty-eight technical advisors and a cast that included John Wayne, Robert Mitchum, Henry Fonda, Richard Burton, and Sean Connery. It cost $10 million and earned $40 million at the box office.

As head of Twentieth Century Fox he went on to oversee The Sound of Music (1965), Planet of the Apes (1968), and Tora! Tora! Tora! (1970). He died in Palm Springs, California, in 1979.

The campaign: a building for the collection

The Signal Corps Museum on post closed on 25 February 2021, when base growth and construction took its building. The collection — the artifacts behind every room in this virtual museum — has been in storage ever since.

The Society is raising funds to buy a building just outside the gates of Fort Eisenhower and turn it back into a museum. $250,000 has been donated so far by more than 80 donors. Once the building is secured, renovation follows, then the exhibits, then the doors open to the public again. Preserving this history matters for training today's Soldiers, educating the public, and preparing young Americans for service.

Civil War Annex

The Civil War Signal Annex

Flags by day, torches by night, and wires to Washington: how Signal officers and telegraph operators made sure the message got through. Then try the code yourself.

Flags and torches

1856–1860

A doctor's idea

The Signal Corps began with an Army doctor. Assistant Surgeon Albert J. Myer proposed a system of visual signaling in 1856 while serving as a medical officer in Texas. He called it wigwag. On June 21, 1860, Congress authorized one signal officer for the Army, and Myer was named to the post with the rank of major. For a time he was the Corps' only member.

Day and night

How a message crossed the field

By day a signalman waved a flag. At night he waved a torch. Early in the war the motions stood for the numerals 1 through 4, which were grouped to mean letters, words or phrases. By the war's end a simpler two-motion code, close in spirit to telegraph dots and dashes, did the same job. A signal disk of two turning circles let operators change the code so that enemy watchers could not read it as easily.

1861–1865

Line of sight and a small crew

Wig-wag needed a clear line of sight, so signalmen built relay stations within view of each other, and it needed good weather and many well-trained Soldiers. A signal party could be as small as one officer and two privates, and only the officer knew the code. Signalmen were chosen by examination, and officers were tested in subjects that included reading, arithmetic, chemistry and surveying. During the Battle of Gettysburg, wig-wag relayed updates to Washington, D.C.

June 1861

The first test under fire

Wig-wag's first test in combat came in June 1861, early in the Civil War. Signalmen used it to direct the fire of a harbor battery against Confederate positions near Fort Monroe, Virginia.

1861–1863

Getting the message through

When wires could not go, signal officers did. At First Bull Run, Capt. E. Porter Alexander signaled from Signal Hill that Col. Nathan Evans's position was being turned, and reinforcements followed. At Gettysburg on July 1, 1863, Lt. Aaron B. Jerome watched from church and courthouse towers. When Longstreet's corps spotted the signal station on Little Round Top, it delayed its attack with a counter march. On July 3, Capt. Edward C. Pierce rode as a courier to Meade's headquarters despite warnings.

Sept 1862

A warning from Elk Mountain

At the Battle of Antietam, the Union's most important signal station stood on Elk Mountain, east of the battlefield. Lt. Joseph Gloskoski reported contact with at least five stations during the fighting. One message credited to his station warned General Burnside: “Look well to your left. The enemy are moving a strong force in that direction.” It referred to A.P. Hill's division approaching.

1861–1865

One code, two armies

Both armies used the same system. E. Porter Alexander, Myer's assistant, resigned his Army commission to join the Confederacy and helped organize, equip and train the Confederate Signal Service. The result, as the National Park Service puts it, was that both sides ended up with essentially the same signaling method.

Mar 3, 1863

From one officer to a Corps

On March 3, 1863, Congress authorized a regular Signal Corps for the duration of the war. About 2,900 officers and enlisted men served in it across the Civil War, though not all at the same time. Even then the telegraph was beginning to replace wig-wag.

Wires and keys

1837–1844

Morse, Vail and the dot-and-dash code

Samuel F. B. Morse needed technical help, money and political support to turn his telegraph into a working system. Working with the technician Alfred Vail, he developed the system of dots and dashes that became known as Morse code, around 1837. On May 24, 1844, Morse sent “What hath God wrought?” from the Supreme Court room in the U.S. Capitol to Vail in Baltimore.

1862–1865

Lincoln in the telegraph office

In March 1862 the War Department set up its telegraph office in the old library of the War Department building, next to the White House. President Lincoln spent more time there than anywhere outside the White House, often walking over at night, and he slept on a cot there during key battles. He read the incoming “lightning messages” at a desk by the window while operators turned Morse code into written messages.

1861–1865

Civilians on the wires

The telegraph that served Lincoln was run by civilians. The U.S. Military Telegraph Corps, headed from October 1861 by former Western Union official Anson Stager, grew to more than 1,500 people and built about 15,000 miles of field, land and submarine line. Only its supervisors held military commissions. Operators received no military recognition until 1897. Important messages were sent in cipher.

1862–1863

The Signal Corps' own telegraph

The Signal Corps tried to run its own electric telegraph. Its Beardslee field telegraph used a hand-cranked generator and a dial instead of a key, so it did not send Morse code. It reached only about 5 to 8 miles, and its pointers often fell out of step. At Fredericksburg on December 13, 1862, signal corpsmen ran a line across the Rappahannock River under fire. At Chancellorsville in May 1863, after poor results, Military Telegraph operators replaced the Signal Corps' Beardslee operators.

The Satellite Annex

From a radar echo off the Moon to Soldiers training on satellite systems at Fort Gordon: how the Signal Corps took communications into space.

Jan 10, 1946

The Moon as a mirror

On January 10, 1946, Signal Corps engineers at Evans Signal Laboratory in New Jersey aimed a radar at the rising Moon and waited. About two and a half seconds later, the echo came back. Project Diana is cited by Army historians as showing that communication through space was feasible.

Dec 18, 1958

SCORE: a voice from orbit

On December 18, 1958, an Air Force Atlas rocket placed SCORE, Signal Communications by Orbiting Relay Equipment, in orbit. Signal Corps laboratories at Fort Monmouth, New Jersey, built its communications package for the Advanced Research Projects Agency. A tape recorder carried President Eisenhower's Christmas greeting. The experiment ran about twelve days before the batteries failed, and SCORE re-entered the atmosphere on January 21, 1959. It is credited as the first communications satellite.

Oct 4, 1960

Courier 1B: a message in storage

Courier 1B, launched October 4, 1960, was a military communications satellite that Army historians describe as the Army's own. A 51-inch sphere covered in 19,200 solar cells, it stored voice and telegraph messages and relayed them at about 67,000 words a minute, working with ground stations at Fort Monmouth and in Puerto Rico. In a single 14-minute pass it relayed the text of the King James Bible, 773,693 words. Its communications failed after 17 days.

1960–1988

Running the Army's satellite effort

In September 1960 the Army set up an Advent Management Agency at Fort Monmouth, the first U.S. military agency to manage satellite communications. By mid-1962 its roles were redefined into the U.S. Army Satellite Communications Agency, which handled ground terminals for all the military services. The agency was disestablished in October 1988, and its duties passed to three program offices.

Jul 1969

Bringing Apollo 11 home

When Apollo 11 returned from the Moon in July 1969, Army satellite terminals helped carry its communications home. The Army Satellite Communications Agency led a joint effort of the Army, Navy, Air Force and Marines that used tactical satellite terminals as the recovery force's first line of communications. TACSAT I, an experimental satellite launched in February 1969, carried the re-entry and splashdown voice link through the Army-developed AN/TRC-156 terminal.

1973–today

Where Soldiers learn satellite communications

In April 1973 the Army announced that the Signal School would move from Fort Monmouth, New Jersey, to Fort Gordon. When the last Monmouth class graduated in June 1976, the school's training focused on operating, repairing, managing and engineering satellite and computerized systems for worldwide communications. In the 1990s Fort Gordon became the Defense Department's main training site for most satellite operators and maintainers. The Signal School's course list today includes 25S, Satellite Communication Systems Specialist.

1995–2007

From DSCS to Wideband Global SATCOM

On May 1, 1995, the Army created the 1st Satellite Control Battalion, its first battalion with an operational mission tied to space systems, to plan and control the networks and payloads of Defense Satellite Communications System satellites. The first Wideband Global SATCOM satellite launched on October 10, 2007, and Army historians report that one WGS satellite offers 12 times the capacity of a legacy DSCS satellite. On the tactical side, the Joint Network Node began fielding in 2004 to give battalion-level and higher units satellite connectivity.

The Weather Annex

From Army surgeons' weather diaries to the forecast on your phone: from 1870 to 1891 the Signal Corps was the nation's weather service, and it kept working on Army weather long after.

The line of descent

The Signal Service years

1814–1873

Army doctors keep the first weather diaries

The Army was recording weather long before it had a weather service. In 1814 the Surgeon General had Army post and hospital surgeons keep weather diaries, because doctors wanted to understand how weather and disease were connected. The National Archives calls this the first nationwide federal weather system. The Smithsonian began gathering weather reports from volunteer observers in 1847, and in 1873 those observers were handed over to the Signal Corps.

1870

Congress hands the job to the Army

In 1870 a series of freak storms killed several people on the Great Lakes. Congress responded with a law, approved February 9, 1870, directing the War Department to take weather observations at military posts and share them by telegraph. A Wisconsin congressman, Halbert Paine, wrote the measure and argued that military discipline would give the quickest, most regular and most accurate reports. On March 15, 1870, the job went to the Chief Signal Officer, Albert J. Myer, and the Signal Corps became the nation's weather service.

1870–1880

Myer builds the network

Myer trained signalmen to run the new service, and soldiers took observations at stations across the country, with reports sent by telegraph. According to the Army's concise history, there were 25 regular stations in 1870 and 60 the next year, and one of them was in Augusta, Georgia. By the time Myer died in 1880 the service was world renowned. In the 1880s weather work took up nearly all of the Signal Corps' effort.

1880–1881

From Myer to the Arctic

Myer died in 1880. In 1881 Lt. Adolphus Greely led a Signal Corps party to the Arctic to take part in the First International Polar Year. Their station at Lady Franklin Bay, which the Army also calls Fort Conger, was the northernmost polar site that year. Greely later supervised the handover of the weather service to the Department of Agriculture.

1890–1891

The weather goes civilian

In 1890 Congress decided the nation's weather service should be civilian. President Benjamin Harrison signed the law on October 1, 1890, creating the Weather Bureau in the Department of Agriculture. The Signal Corps' weather network moved to the new bureau, and the change took effect on July 1, 1891. The Army sources say the Signal Corps kept responsibility for military weather work, and by then it operated about five hundred observation stations.

Weather for the Army

1917–1918

The Army builds a Meteorological Service for the war

When the United States entered World War I, the Army needed weather forecasts for , artillery and gas attacks. In September 1917 Maj. William R. Blair went to France to organize a Signal Corps Meteorological Service. The first American weather station in France opened in May 1918, and by October 1918 there were twenty-two. By the end of the war the Army had trained about five hundred weathermen.

1919–1937

Weather moves to the Air Corps

After the war most of the trained weathermen left the Army. Capt. Randolph “Pinkie” Williams argued for a permanent military weather service, and in 1935 the Chief Signal Officer expanded the Corps' Meteorological Section. In 1937 a committee led by the Secretary of War recommended that the Air Corps run weather service in wartime. On July 1, 1937, the Meteorological Section moved from the Signal Corps to the Army Air Corps, which created the Army Air Corps Weather Service. The Signal Corps kept the job of developing and supplying weather equipment. In 1947, when the Air Force became a separate service, Air Force Weather took over weather support for both the Air Force and the Army.

1940s–1961

The Signal Corps' instruments reach space

Even after the weather forecasters left, the Signal Corps kept building the instruments. In the 1940s its laboratories used radar to track storms. On April 1, 1960, the first TIROS weather satellite was launched. The Signal Corps provided its meteorological instruments, including the television cameras, and Fort Monmouth hosted one of the two ground stations that received its pictures. TIROS-1 worked for 78 days and sent back nearly 23,000 cloud pictures, showing for the first time that satellites could survey the world's weather. 1940–1970

What became of the Weather Bureau

The Weather Bureau that grew out of the Signal Corps stayed civilian. It moved to the Department of Commerce in 1940, became part of the Environmental Science Services Administration in 1965, and in 1970 joined the new National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. The National Weather Service inside NOAA is its direct successor, so the forecast on your phone traces back to Army signalmen taking observations in 1870.

Fort Gordon

Fort Gordon, Then and Now

From the 1898 camps around Georgia to today's Cyber Center of Excellence: where the post has stood, what it has been called, the people its name and its buildings remember, and the Navy ships that carried Augusta's name.

Gallery I · Roots

Before Fort Gordon: 1898 to 1919

Georgia trained more soldiers for the Spanish-American War than almost any state, with more than twenty-five camps spread from Athens and Atlanta to Augusta, Columbus and Macon. In Augusta, troops of the Army's Second Corps camped in late 1898 at a site first called Camp Young and renamed Camp MacKenzie by November. It was abandoned in February 1899.

The name "Camp Gordon" first appeared on the coast that same summer. A short-lived tent camp by that name stood near the St. Simons Island lighthouse, where companies of the 2nd North Carolina Volunteer Infantry guarded St. Simons Sound from August to mid-September 1898.

When the United States entered World War I, a far larger Camp Gordon rose in Chamblee, northeast of Atlanta. The cantonment trained the 82nd "All American" Division and Sergeant Alvin York, and held more than 46,000 troops at its peak before closing in 1919. Augusta had its own World War I post, Camp Hancock, on the same ground used in 1898. Today the Chamblee site is DeKalb-Peachtree Airport.

Augusta's Fort Gordon is not a continuation of the Chamblee camp. From 1919 to 1941 no Army post named Camp Gordon existed anywhere.

Gallery II · Augusta, 1941

A Camp Built for War

By May 1941 the Army had chosen land in Richmond County, southwest of Augusta, for a new division training camp, and construction began that July. The camp took the old name, Camp Gordon. Pearl Harbor forced it open early: on December 9, 1941 the commander moved his staff from a temporary office in Augusta's post office into an unfinished headquarters building.

The 4th Infantry Division was the first unit assigned. The 26th Infantry Division and the 10th Armored Division followed. Among the early arrivals were the Comanche code talkers of the 4th Signal Company, who reached Camp Gordon on December 29, 1941.

From October 1943 to January 1945 the camp held prisoners of war. After victory it became a separation center that discharged roughly 86,000 soldiers.

Gallery III · 1948 to today

From Signal Corps to Cyber

Camp Gordon nearly closed after the war. The Cold War saved it. The Military Police School arrived from Carlisle Barracks in September 1948, and a Signal Corps Training Center opened on October 1, 1948. On March 21, 1956 the camp became a permanent installation and took the name Fort Gordon.

Infantry, military police and signal soldiers trained here through the Vietnam era. In 1974 the Army moved its Signal School from Fort Monmouth, New Jersey, and consolidated all communications training at Fort Gordon. The post became home of the Signal Corps Regiment in June 1986.

In March 2014 Fort Gordon was designated the U.S. Army Cyber Center of Excellence, with the Signal and Cyber Schools under one command. A new cyber campus now stands where , the ten-story landmark dedicated in 1970, once rose. About 20,000 signal and cyber soldiers train here each year.

Gallery VI · Sea Service

The Navy, Augusta and the Ships That Carried Its Name

Augusta's name has gone to sea six times. The first ship, a brig bought in 1799, was named for the Georgia city and fought in the Quasi-War with France. The most famous, the cruiser USS Augusta (CA-31), was christened by Miss Evelyn McDaniel of Augusta and served from 1931 to 1946. Few ships in American history carried so many of the people who shaped the Second World War.

The Navy is part of Fort Gordon's story too. Sailors have served on post since November 1, 1995, when the Naval Security Group Activity Fort Gordon was commissioned. Renamed Navy Information Operations Command Georgia in 2005, it supplies Navy cryptologic specialists to NSA/CSS Georgia and to the fleet. A Navy training detachment on post helps sailors prepare for advancement.

Ships named Augusta

1799–1801

Brig Augusta

Named for Augusta, Georgia. In the Quasi-War she escorted a convoy to the Caribbean and captured several French privateers, working off Haiti alongside Toussaint Louverture's forces in 1800.

1861–1868

Side-wheel steamer Augusta

Bought at the start of the Civil War. She joined the Port Royal expedition in November 1861, blockaded Savannah and Charleston, and in January 1863 fought the Confederate ironclads Chicora and Palmetto State off Charleston.

1917–1918

Patrol yacht Augusta (SP-946)

A private steam yacht that kept its owner's name while on loan to the Navy in World War I.

1931–1946 Featured

Cruiser Augusta (CA-31)

Named for Augusta, Georgia. Asiatic Fleet flagship, Atlantic Charter conference ship, Torch and Normandy flagship, and the ship that carried President Truman to Potsdam. Three battle stars.

1985–2009

Submarine Augusta (SSN-710)

A Los Angeles-class attack submarine named for Augusta, Maine. She fired Tomahawk missiles in the opening of Operation Iraqi Freedom in March 2003.

2023–today

Littoral combat ship Augusta (LCS-34)

Commissioned September 30, 2023 in Eastport, Maine, and named for Maine's capital.

The cruiser's war

1933–1940 · Asiatic Fleet

Flagship of the Asiatic Fleet. Off Shanghai in August 1937 she watched the Sino-Japanese fighting from the Bund, and a stray shell killed one of her sailors. Survivors of the gunboat Panay, sunk by Japanese planes that December, spent Christmas aboard.

August 1941 · Placentia Bay

President Roosevelt sailed in her to Newfoundland to meet Prime Minister Churchill. Their talks, held aboard naval vessels in the bay, produced the Atlantic Charter.

November 1942 · North Africa

Flagship of Rear Adm. H. Kent Hewitt for Operation Torch, carrying Maj. Gen. George S. Patton. Her guns sank the French destroyer Le Boulonnais off Fedhala.

June 1944 · Normandy

Flagship of Rear Adm. Alan G. Kirk, with Lt. Gen. Omar Bradley and his staff aboard. She fired on D-Day and drove off German aircraft off Omaha Beach.

August 1944 · Southern France

Bombarded coastal batteries for Operation Dragoon. Her Marines, with those of USS Philadelphia, took the German surrender of the islands off Marseille.

July 1945 · Potsdam

Carried President Truman, Secretary of State James Byrnes and Fleet Adm. William Leahy to Europe for the Potsdam Conference. She then brought servicemen home in Operation Magic Carpet.

Master Sergeant Gary Ivan Gordon

October 3, 1993

When the Black Hawk "Super Six Four" was shot down, Gordon and his teammate, Sergeant First Class Randall Shughart, were providing sniper cover from a helicopter overhead. They volunteered to go in on foot to protect the crew. Twice their request to be put on the ground was refused; on the third request they got permission.

Inserted about 100 meters from the crash, the two fought through enemy fire and a dense maze of shanties to reach the wreck. They set up a perimeter around the aircraft, taking the most exposed positions, and kept moving along it to protect the downed crew. Shughart fell first; Gordon fought on until his rifle ammunition was gone, and was then killed. The pilot, Chief Warrant Officer Michael Durant, was captured and released about two weeks later. He has said he owes his life to the two men.

The Medal of Honor

Gordon and Shughart received the Medal of Honor posthumously, presented to their widows by President Clinton at the White House. They were the only soldiers in the operation to receive it.

Remembered

The Navy named the cargo ship USNS Gordon for him in 1996. Fort Devens dedicated Gordon Hall in 2023. Since June 11, 2025, Fort Gordon itself honors him, along with Master Sergeant Gary Gordon Boulevard and the planned Gary Gordon Plaza.

Clayton Barracks

Spc. Hilda I. Clayton

An Augusta native and Army combat camera specialist with the 55th Signal Company, Clayton was killed at 22 on July 2, 2013, in Laghman Province, Afghanistan, when a mortar tube exploded during a training exercise she was photographing. Chibitty Hall

Cpl. Charles Chibitty

One of seventeen Comanche soldiers of the 4th Signal Company recruited to build a code from their language, Chibitty arrived at Camp Gordon with the code talkers on December 29, 1941 for radio training. The Comanche code was never broken.

He landed at Utah Beach in June 1944 and sent his first message in Comanche. He was the last surviving Comanche code talker when he received the Knowlton Award at the Pentagon in 1999.

Eisenhower Army Medical Center

Gen. of the Army Dwight D. Eisenhower

Eisenhower, Augusta and the Army hospital

Dwight D. Eisenhower, Supreme Allied Commander in Europe and the 34th President, came to Augusta again and again to golf at Augusta National, to hunt and to rest. Augusta National members built a cabin for him and Mamie. By his own account, Fort Gordon's soldiers helped him, his family and his friends on those trips for years.

On January 7, 1961, days before leaving office, he reviewed Fort Gordon troops on Barton Field and used the moment to say goodbye to the Army he had joined fifty years earlier.

"This is the last review that I shall ever receive in my life."Remarks at Troop Review Ceremonies, Fort Gordon, January 7, 1961 (American Presidency Project)

The post hospital began in 1941 as the Camp Gordon Station Hospital. Its current building opened in 1976 and carries his name: the Dwight D. Eisenhower Army Medical Center. When the post's name returned to Gordon in 2025, the Army said Eisenhower would still be honored on the installation, including through the hospital.

Lt. Col. Aquilla J. “Jimmie” Dyess, USMC

An Augusta native and Eagle Scout, Dyess won the Carnegie Medal in 1929 for helping save swimmers off Charleston. As a Marine battalion commander he was killed leading the assault on Roi-Namur, Kwajalein Atoll, in 1944 and received the Medal of Honor posthumously.

He remains the only American awarded both the Carnegie Medal and the Medal of Honor. Georgia named the road to Fort Gordon's main gate for him on October 20, 1994.

Sources: Georgia DOT, Resolution 1994-35-TB; Augusta-Richmond County, Monuments.

Master Sergeant Gary Gordon Boulevard

Master Sgt. Gary I. Gordon

Dedicated with the post on September 26, 2025. The boulevard leads to the planned Gary Gordon Plaza in front of the new Cyber Center of Excellence headquarters.

Source: Army.mil, September 2025.
Signal barracks, dedicated 2019–2020

Edgerton barracks

Sgt. Marshall L. Edgerton

A Whitfield County, Georgia native serving with the 82nd Airborne Division, Edgerton died in Iraq in 2003 in a suicide bombing; his response prevented mass casualties at a dining facility. He was promoted to sergeant posthumously and received the Bronze Star and Purple Heart.

Fort Gordon named its largest signal barracks in his memory.

Source: Dalton Citizen, January 6, 2020.
10th Street at Chamberlain Ave.

Topham Training Center

Sgt. Andrew Topham

Topham set up communications networks in the Middle East after the September 11 attacks, then taught as an instructor at Fort Gordon and worked as an Army civilian with the 93rd Signal Brigade. The center is an East Coast regional IT training facility.

Source: Army Scholarship Foundation.
Opened 1966 · name retired 2019 · no longer standing

Greely Hall

Maj. Gen. Adolphus Greely

Greely Hall opened in 1966 in the first Signal School reorganization and taught generations of signal soldiers. Its namesake was the Signal Corps' fifth Medal of Honor recipient.

The building came down for the new cyber campus; the plaque was removed and the name retired at a 2019 ceremony attended by Greely's descendants.

Sources: Army.mil, cyber campus groundbreaking; DVIDS, cyber campus milestone.
Built 1968 · dedicated 1970 · closed 2021 · no longer standing

Signal Towers

The Signal Regiment

The ten-story landmark was dedicated on August 15, 1970 and once housed the Signal Corps Museum along with a cafeteria, bank, bookstore and barbershop. Its doors closed on July 31, 2021, and it was memorialized in April 2022 before demolition for the cyber campus.

Sources: Army.mil, April 2022; WJBF.
August 1941 · Placentia Bay

Franklin D. Roosevelt

President of the United States

Roosevelt boarded Augusta on August 5, 1941 and sailed to Placentia Bay, Newfoundland. Prime Minister Winston Churchill arrived on August 9.

Over the following days the two leaders met aboard the ships anchored in the bay and agreed on the Atlantic Charter, a joint statement of war aims issued on August 14, four months before the United States entered the war.

Sources: Naval History and Heritage Command, Augusta (CA-31); National Archives, FDR Library.
August 1941 · Placentia Bay

Winston Churchill

Prime Minister of the United Kingdom

Churchill arrived at Placentia Bay on August 9, 1941 to meet Roosevelt. The conferences on August 11 and 12, held between Augusta and the British battleship HMS Prince of Wales, produced the Atlantic Charter.

Sources: Naval History and Heritage Command, Augusta (CA-31); National Archives, FDR Library.
1941 · Atlantic Fleet

Ernest J. King

Admiral, Commander in Chief, Atlantic Fleet

King made Augusta his flagship as Commander in Chief, Atlantic Fleet, in May 1941. Three months later she carried President Roosevelt to the Atlantic Charter conference.

Source: Naval History and Heritage Command, Augusta (CA-31).
1933–1935 · Asiatic Fleet

Chester W. Nimitz

Captain, commanding officer

Before he led the Pacific Fleet to victory in World War II, Nimitz commanded Augusta. He took her to the Far East, where she served as flagship of the Asiatic Fleet under his command.

Source: Naval History and Heritage Command, Nimitz biography.
November 1942 · Operation Torch

George S. Patton Jr.

Major General, Western Task Force

Patton sailed to North Africa aboard Augusta, flagship of Rear Adm. H. Kent Hewitt. On November 8, 1942 the cruiser bombarded shore batteries off Fedhala, Morocco, and fought French warships during the landings.

Source: Naval History and Heritage Command, Augusta (CA-31).
June 1944 · Normandy

Omar N. Bradley

Lieutenant General, First U.S. Army

Bradley and his staff came aboard on June 5, 1944. He watched the D-Day landings from Augusta, flagship of Rear Adm. Alan G. Kirk, as she fired in support of the troops and drove off German aircraft over Omaha Beach.

Source: Naval History and Heritage Command, Augusta (CA-31).
May 1944 and August 1945

King George VI

King of the United Kingdom

The King visited Augusta on May 25, 1944 as the Normandy invasion force gathered, and came aboard again on August 2, 1945, while the ship was carrying President Truman home from Potsdam.

Source: Naval History and Heritage Command, Augusta (CA-31).
July–August 1945 · Potsdam

Harry S. Truman

President of the United States

Truman embarked on July 7, 1945 with Secretary of State James Byrnes and Fleet Adm. William Leahy. Augusta landed them at Antwerp on July 14 for the Potsdam Conference and brought the President home in August.

Sources: Naval History and Heritage Command, Augusta (CA-31); National Archives, Truman Library photo 63-1382-52.
1945

Dwight D. Eisenhower

General of the Army

Eisenhower was among the dignitaries Augusta received during the Potsdam voyage of 1945.

Source: Naval History and Heritage Command, Augusta (CA-31).
