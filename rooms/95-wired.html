The Wired Communications Annex

Telegraph, telephone, field wire and cable: the lines the Signal Corps strung behind every army, and the wiremen and linemen who laid them and went back out to mend them.

1861 to 1904

From the flying telegraph to Alaska

1861–1862

The flying telegraph train

In August 1861 Albert Myer was authorized a telegraph train of horse-drawn wagons. Each train carried five miles of wire in two wagons. It had its first field test on the Virginia Peninsula in May 1862, with the hand-cranked Beardslee instrument. The maps where the wires went.

1874–1891

Wires across the frontier

An 1874 law ordered military telegraph lines to link the Army's posts in Texas, and lines in Arizona, New Mexico and the Northwest followed. The mileage in service peaked at 5,077 miles in 1881. In all, the Signal Corps was responsible for building 8,000 miles of telegraph line.

1880s–1892

The telephone joins the Army

Sgt. Adolf Eccard devised a combined field telephone and telegraph, but at $500 it cost too much. In 1892 Capt. Charles Kilbourne built a cable cart that let one man lay two miles of wire. By then half of all Army posts had telephones.

1898

Wire to the front in Cuba

Without horses, signalmen carried coils of wire on their backs or loaded them on pack mules, and laid insulated wire on the ground and over bushes. President McKinley's war room in the White House had a telegraph link to Cuba, and General Shafter could reach Washington within twenty minutes.

1898–1907

The buzzer

Buzzer lines sent Morse code through telephone receivers and worked even over bare or poorly insulated wire. In 1907 Maj. Saltzman wrote that “the most important enlisted man in the fifty-mile battle line of the future will be the man behind the buzzer.”

1900–1904

The Alaska system

For the Washington–Alaska Military Cable and Telegraph System, Lt. Billy Mitchell surveyed and built the Eagle-to-Valdez line of about 420 miles, traveling more than 2,000 miles by dog sled. The finished system had 2,079 miles of submarine cable, 1,439 miles of land line and a 107-mile wireless link, 3,625 miles in all.

1917 to 1918

Wire in the Great War

1917–1918

Wire in the trenches

At the front, lines ran on four-foot stakes or along trench walls, and trunk lines were buried. A soldier's breast reel held about half a mile of light twisted pair, which the men nicknamed “please don't rain” wire. The Army used twenty thousand miles of outpost wire a month.

1917–1918

A telephone network for an army

By the Armistice the Signal Corps had built more than 1,700 miles of permanent pole line in France and strung nearly 23,000 miles of wire. Women telephone operators worked exchanges in Paris, Chaumont and seventy-five other French towns. Their story is in the room.

1932 to today

Nine hundred thousand miles, and after

1932–1942

The tools of World War II

The EE-8 field telephone weighed about ten pounds and was standardized in 1932. The BD-71 switchboard took six lines and the BD-72 twelve. Spiral-four cable became standard in February 1942, alongside W-110 field wire and the lighter W-130 assault wire.

1942–1943

The Alaska Highway line

Civilian crews and the 843d Signal Service Battalion built an open-wire telephone line along the new Alaska Highway in fifteen months. It took 95,000 poles and 14,000 miles of wire.

1944–1945

Nine hundred thousand miles

In the pursuit across France the armies used nearly three thousand miles of wire a day. In eleven months in the European Theater, Signal Corps soldiers laid more than 900,000 miles of field wire.

1950 onward

Lighter wire, then glass

Korea brought lighter field wire, and airplanes laid wire over ground too rough to cross on foot. In South Vietnam field wire saw relatively little use. Fiber-optic cable followed: smaller, lighter and immune to electromagnetic pulse. Wire's partner in the air is in the .

A tribute

Ode to the Wireman and the Lineman

Written for the museum

They went out first, ahead of the order,
a reel on the chest and a coil on the shoulder,
and paid out the line through the mud and the dark
so that one voice could find another.

No one salutes a strand of wire.
It lies in the ditch, it hangs from a thorn,
until a shell finds it. Then someone goes,
alone, with pliers and tape, to the break.

Climb, lineman. The pole is cold,
the spurs bite deep, and the sky is watching.
Splice, wireman. Hand over hand,
follow the line back into the fire.

From the buzzer in Cuba to the cable in Alaska snow,
from the stakes of the trenches to the roads of France,
across rivers swum with a wire at the waist,
the Army spoke because you were there.

So here is your monument. It is not bronze.
It is a line that held, a call that went through,
a commander who heard, a gun that answered,
and the word, at last, that someone got home.

What the record says of them

In World War I the Signal Corps was only about 4 percent of the American Expeditionary Forces, yet its 2,840 casualties ranked second only to the Infantry's.

Raines, Getting the Message Through, chapter 5

At Paco Bridge in Manila on February 5, 1899, 1st Lt. Charles E. Kilbourne Jr. climbed a telegraph pole under enemy fire to repair a broken wire. He received the Medal of Honor.

Raines, chapter 3

Near Montrefagne, France, on October 9, 1918, Sgt. Clifton Burnett “repeatedly repaired telephone wires when no linemen were available,” although four of the men working with him were wounded. He received the Distinguished Service Cross.

National Archives, photo 111-SC-93658

In Italy in the winter of 1943–44, linemen “waded and swam across the mountain torrents.” Crews tied a length of wire or cable around a volunteer's waist and wished him good luck.

CMH, The Signal Corps: The Outcome

At Buna, in New Guinea, Col. Auchincloss picked up a lineman's kit and said, “I'm going out to fix that line. Any of you people want to come along?” Every man followed.

CMH, The Signal Corps: The Outcome

In Burma in 1944, “the Japanese, having broken the wire, lay in wait till the lineman came to make repairs.”
