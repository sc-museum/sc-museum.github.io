Wireless Radio Annex

The Wireless Radio Annex

From a spark across twelve miles of water in 1899 to the networked radio on a paratrooper's back: how the Signal Corps took the Army's voice off the wire.

1899 to 1918

Sparks, static and the first voices

April 1899

The Army's first wireless link

Capt. George Squier and Lt. Col. James Allen built a wireless telegraph system that was first used between Fire Island, New York, and the Fire Island lightship, about twelve miles away. Links in New York Harbor and across San Francisco Bay, from Fort Mason to Alcatraz, followed.

1904

Wireless across Norton Sound

In Alaska, ice floes made a submarine cable impossible between St. Michael and Nome. Capt. Leonard Wildman joined them by radio across 107 miles of water. The Chief Signal Officer, Adolphus Greely, called it the longest wireless section of any commercial telegraph system in the world. The rest of the Alaska system is in the .

Spring 1918

Radio laboratories at Camp Vail

Radio research outgrew its space and moved to Camp Alfred Vail at Little Silver, New Jersey, the post later known as Fort Monmouth. A squadron there flew nearly one hundred test flights a week.

1918

Voices from the cockpit

The Signal Corps' chief wartime advance was the airborne radiotelephone, which let a pilot talk with the ground. President and Mrs. Wilson spoke with a pilot flying over the White House. The sets reached France too late to be used in combat. The fliers' story is in the .

1918

Listening to the enemy

Signal Corps soldiers copied German coded radio traffic and used goniometry, or direction finding, to take bearings on enemy transmitters and locate them. False American radio traffic helped mislead the Germans before the Meuse attack of November 1, 1918.

1918

Armstrong in Paris

Edwin H. Armstrong, commissioned a captain in the Signal Corps, began developing the superheterodyne receiver at the Corps' laboratory in Paris. It greatly amplified weak signals and allowed precise tuning, though he could not perfect it before the Armistice. He later became known as the father of FM radio.

1934 to 1945

Radio goes to war

1934–1939

The first walkie-talkie

The SCR-194 and SCR-195 were the first sets called walkie-talkies: AM radios of about twenty-five pounds with a range of up to five miles. They were field tested in 1934 and went into production in 1939.

1936–1941

Radar, and the warning at Opana

In December 1936 the Signal Corps Laboratories' radar detected an airplane seven miles away at Newark, New Jersey. On December 7, 1941, at Opana on Oahu, Pvts. Elliott and Lockard saw a large echo about 130 miles out at 7:02 in the morning. They reported it and were told to “forget it.”

1942–1944

Walkie-talkie and handie-talkie

The SCR-300 walkie-talkie was an FM set of about thirty-five pounds, carried on the back, with a range of about two miles. American troops first used it in combat at Anzio. The five-pound AM SCR-536, the handie-talkie, could be held and worked with one hand. Armored units used the 500 series of FM sets and the Field Artillery the 600 series.

1942–1944

Long range, and radio relay

The SCR-299, carried in a truck and trailer and designed for “100 miles in motion,” linked Oran in North Africa with England. Radio relay, which the soldiers called “antrac,” was first used in North Africa. The Army's historian called it the marriage of wire and radio.

1965 to today

Transistors, hopping and networks

1965–1988

From the PRC-25 to SINCGARS

The transistorized FM AN/PRC-25 gave the infantry 920 channels. The Army's historian wrote that it had the greatest impact on communications of any item of equipment in the Vietnam War, and the AN/PRC-77 followed it. SINCGARS, a radio that hops between frequencies, began reaching units in 1988 with the 2d Infantry Division in Korea.

2021

Networked radios for small units

In January 2021 paratroopers of the 82nd Airborne Division tested two-channel networked radios at Fort Bragg: the Leader Radio, AN/PRC-163, and the Manpack, AN/PRC-162.
