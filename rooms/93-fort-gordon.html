Fort Gordon

Fort Gordon, Then and Now

From the 1898 camps around Georgia to today's Cyber Center of Excellence: where the post has stood, what it has been called, and the people its name and its buildings remember.

Gallery I · Roots

Before Fort Gordon: 1898 to 1919

Georgia trained more soldiers for the Spanish-American War than almost any state, with more than twenty-five camps spread from Athens and Atlanta to Augusta, Columbus and Macon. In Augusta, troops of the Army's Second Corps camped in late 1898 at a site first called Camp Young and renamed Camp MacKenzie by November. It was abandoned in February 1899.

The name "Camp Gordon" first appeared on the coast that same summer. A short-lived tent camp by that name stood near the St. Simons Island lighthouse, where companies of the 2nd North Carolina Volunteer Infantry guarded St. Simons Sound from August to mid-September 1898.

When the United States entered World War I, a far larger Camp Gordon rose in Chamblee, northeast of Atlanta. The cantonment trained the 82nd "All American" Division and Sergeant Alvin York, and held more than 46,000 troops at its peak before closing in 1919. Augusta had its own World War I post, Camp Hancock, on the same ground used in 1898. Today the Chamblee site is DeKalb-Peachtree Airport.

Augusta's Fort Gordon is not a continuation of the Chamblee camp. From 1919 to 1941 no Army post named Camp Gordon existed anywhere.

Gallery II · Augusta, 1941

A Camp Built for War

By May 1941 the Army had chosen land in Richmond County, southwest of Augusta, for a new division training camp, and construction began that July. The camp took the old name, Camp Gordon. Pearl Harbor forced it open early: on December 9, 1941 the commander moved his staff from a temporary office in Augusta's post office into an unfinished headquarters building.

The 4th Infantry Division was the first unit assigned. The 26th Infantry Division and the 10th Armored Division followed. Among the early arrivals were the Comanche code talkers of the 4th Signal Company, who reached Camp Gordon on December 29, 1941.

From October 1943 to January 1945 the camp held prisoners of war. After victory it became a separation center that discharged roughly 86,000 soldiers.

Sources: U.S. Army, Cyber Center of Excellence history and Fort Gordon history; Fort Gordon MWR, Comanche Code Talkers; Augusta-Richmond County, Monuments; Reese Library, Augusta University, Camp Gordon construction photo; New Georgia Encyclopedia, Fort Gordon.

Gallery III · 1948 to today

From Signal Corps to Cyber

Camp Gordon nearly closed after the war. The Cold War saved it. The Military Police School arrived from Carlisle Barracks in September 1948, and a Signal Corps Training Center opened on October 1, 1948. On March 21, 1956 the camp became a permanent installation and took the name Fort Gordon.

Infantry, military police and signal soldiers trained here through the Vietnam era. In 1974 the Army moved its Signal School from Fort Monmouth, New Jersey, and consolidated all communications training at Fort Gordon. The post became home of the Signal Corps Regiment in June 1986.

In March 2014 Fort Gordon was designated the U.S. Army Cyber Center of Excellence, with the Signal and Cyber Schools under one command. A new cyber campus now stands where , the ten-story landmark dedicated in 1970, once rose. About 20,000 signal and cyber soldiers train here each year.

Sources: U.S. Army, Cyber Center of Excellence history and Fort Gordon history; Army.mil, Signal Towers memorialization, 2022; DVIDS, cyber campus milestone; U.S. Congress, H.Con.Res. 206 (111th); Augusta Tomorrow, city timeline.

Gallery IV · What's in a name

One Name, Three Honorees

The post has carried the Gordon name for most of its life, but the person it honors has changed.

1917

Camp Gordon, Chamblee

Named for John Brown Gordon, a Confederate general who later served as Georgia governor and U.S. senator.

1941

Camp Gordon, Augusta

The new WWII camp takes the same name and the same honoree.

March 21, 1956

Fort Gordon

Made a permanent installation and redesignated a fort.

2021–2022

The Naming Commission

Congress directs a commission to rename posts honoring Confederates. It receives more than 34,000 suggestions and narrows them to 87. Gary Gordon and Randall Shughart appear on that list, as does Eisenhower. Augusta-area representatives proposed the Eisenhower name.

October 2022

Secretary of Defense accepts

Lloyd Austin's memo approves the commission's recommendation of Eisenhower for the Augusta post.

October 27, 2023 Historical name

Fort Eisenhower

Colors cased and uncased on Barton Field, the same field where President Eisenhower reviewed troops in 1961. It is the last of nine posts renamed.

June 10, 2025

Announcement at Fort Bragg

During the Army's 250th birthday remarks, the President announces the return of the Gordon name.

June 11, 2025

Fort Gordon again

Effective immediately, the post is Fort Gordon, now honoring Master Sgt. Gary I. Gordon, Medal of Honor.

September 26, 2025

Dedication ceremony

Formal dedication with his widow, Carmen Drake-Owens, and son Ian. Part of the street at Chamberlain Avenue and Rice Road becomes Master Sergeant Gary Gordon Boulevard, leading to a future Gary Gordon Plaza at the new Cyber Center of Excellence headquarters.

Namesake of Fort Gordon

Master Sergeant Gary Ivan Gordon

Born
August 30, 1960, Lincoln, Maine
Unit
Sniper Team Leader, U.S. Army Special Operations Command, Task Force Ranger
Action
October 3, 1993, Mogadishu, Somalia
Award
Medal of Honor (posthumous), presented May 23, 1994

October 3, 1993

When the Black Hawk "Super Six Four" was shot down, Gordon and his teammate, Sergeant First Class Randall Shughart, were providing sniper cover from a helicopter overhead. They volunteered to go in on foot to protect the crew. Twice their request to be put on the ground was refused; on the third request they got permission.

Inserted about 100 meters from the crash, the two fought through enemy fire and a dense maze of shanties to reach the wreck. They set up a perimeter around the aircraft, taking the most exposed positions, and kept moving along it to protect the downed crew. Shughart fell first; Gordon fought on until his rifle ammunition was gone, and was then killed. The pilot, Chief Warrant Officer Michael Durant, was captured and released about two weeks later. He has said he owes his life to the two men.

The Medal of Honor

Gordon and Shughart received the Medal of Honor posthumously, presented to their widows by President Clinton at the White House. They were the only soldiers in the operation to receive it.

Remembered

The Navy named the cargo ship USNS Gordon for him in 1996. Fort Devens dedicated Gordon Hall in 2023. Since June 11, 2025, Fort Gordon itself honors him, along with Master Sergeant Gary Gordon Boulevard and the planned Gary Gordon Plaza.

"Fort Gordon will hold all the stories he was not able to tell."Carmen Drake-Owens, his widow, at the September 26, 2025 dedication

Soldier Service Center, Bldg. 33720

Darling Hall

1st Lt. John E. Darling Jr.

A West Point graduate, Darling trained at the Signal Officer Basic Course at Fort Gordon before Ranger and Airborne school. After a first assignment with the 82nd Airborne Division, he went to Vietnam and was killed in action after taking command and treating casualties during a combat assault on Fire Support Base Ripcord.

His awards include the Silver Star, Bronze Star and Purple Heart. Every soldier arriving at Fort Gordon in-processes through the building that carries his name, where his photograph and medals hang.

Sources: Augusta Good News, June 2026; Newaygo County GenWeb, biography.
Bldg. 33805, Hospital Road

Clayton Barracks

Spc. Hilda I. Clayton

An Augusta native and Army combat camera specialist with the 55th Signal Company, Clayton was killed at 22 on July 2, 2013, in Laghman Province, Afghanistan, when a mortar tube exploded during a training exercise she was photographing. She was the first Army combat camera soldier to die in Afghanistan.

Her final photograph captured the moment of the blast and was later published by the Army with her family's permission. Fort Gordon named this signal barracks for her on Memorial Day 2019; about 900 soldiers live there each year as they begin their signal careers.

Sources: Army.mil, May 30, 2019; WRDW, May 27, 2019.
Bldg. 21606

Chibitty Hall

Cpl. Charles Chibitty

One of seventeen Comanche soldiers of the 4th Signal Company recruited to build a code from their language, Chibitty arrived at Camp Gordon with the code talkers on December 29, 1941 for radio training. The Comanche code was never broken.

He landed at Utah Beach in June 1944 and sent his first message in Comanche. He was the last surviving Comanche code talker when he received the Knowlton Award at the Pentagon in 1999.

Sources: Army.mil, Charles Chibitty; Fort Gordon MWR, Comanche Code Talkers; U.S. Army, Fort Gordon units page.
300 Hospital Road

Eisenhower Army Medical Center

Gen. of the Army Dwight D. Eisenhower

Eisenhower, Augusta and the Army hospital

Dwight D. Eisenhower, Supreme Allied Commander in Europe and the 34th President, came to Augusta again and again to golf at Augusta National, to hunt and to rest. Augusta National members built a cabin for him and Mamie. By his own account, Fort Gordon's soldiers helped him, his family and his friends on those trips for years.

On January 7, 1961, days before leaving office, he reviewed Fort Gordon troops on Barton Field and used the moment to say goodbye to the Army he had joined fifty years earlier.

"This is the last review that I shall ever receive in my life."Remarks at Troop Review Ceremonies, Fort Gordon, January 7, 1961 (American Presidency Project)

The post hospital began in 1941 as the Camp Gordon Station Hospital. Its current building opened in 1976 and carries his name: the Dwight D. Eisenhower Army Medical Center. When the post's name returned to Gordon in 2025, the Army said Eisenhower would still be honored on the installation, including through the hospital.

Sources: American Presidency Project, Eisenhower's remarks, January 7, 1961; Army.mil, Eisenhower and Barton Field; Augusta Chronicle, Eisenhower's visits; WRDW, hospital keeps his name; Augusta Good News, 2026 heritage program; Wikipedia.
I-20 to Gate 1

Jimmie Dyess Parkway

Lt. Col. Aquilla J. “Jimmie” Dyess, USMC

An Augusta native and Eagle Scout, Dyess won the Carnegie Medal in 1929 for helping save swimmers off Charleston. As a Marine battalion commander he was killed leading the assault on Roi-Namur, Kwajalein Atoll, in 1944 and received the Medal of Honor posthumously.

He remains the only American awarded both the Carnegie Medal and the Medal of Honor. Georgia named the road to Fort Gordon's main gate for him on October 20, 1994.

Edgerton barracks

Sgt. Marshall L. Edgerton

A Whitfield County, Georgia native serving with the 82nd Airborne Division, Edgerton died in Iraq in 2003 in a suicide bombing; his response prevented mass casualties at a dining facility. He was promoted to sergeant posthumously and received the Bronze Star and Purple Heart.

Fort Gordon named its largest signal barracks in his memory.

Source: Dalton Citizen, January 6, 2020.
10th Street at Chamberlain Ave.

Topham Training Center

Sgt. Andrew Topham

Topham set up communications networks in the Middle East after the September 11 attacks, then taught as an instructor at Fort Gordon and worked as an Army civilian with the 93rd Signal Brigade. The center is an East Coast regional IT training facility.

Source: Army Scholarship Foundation.
Opened 1966 · name retired 2019 · no longer standing

Greely Hall

Maj. Gen. Adolphus Greely

Greely Hall opened in 1966 in the first Signal School reorganization and taught generations of signal soldiers. Its namesake was the Signal Corps' fifth Medal of Honor recipient.

The building came down for the new cyber campus; the plaque was removed and the name retired at a 2019 ceremony attended by Greely's descendants.

Sources: Army.mil, cyber campus groundbreaking; DVIDS, cyber campus milestone.
Built 1968 · dedicated 1970 · closed 2021 · no longer standing

Signal Towers

The Signal Regiment

The ten-story landmark was dedicated on August 15, 1970 and once housed the Signal Corps Museum along with a cafeteria, bank, bookstore and barbershop. Its doors closed on July 31, 2021, and it was memorialized in April 2022 before demolition for the cyber campus.
