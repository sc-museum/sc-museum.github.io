The Hello Girls

223 women. One war. A story nearly lost.

In 1918, the U.S. Army Signal Corps sent 223 women to France as telephone operators to help win the First World War. Fluent in French and English and trained on the Army's own switchboards, they connected the calls that moved orders along the front — some working within range of artillery fire. Told through letters, photographs, rare archival footage, and interviews with family and historians, their story remained almost entirely unknown for a century.

An operator’s uniform at the Smithsonian, and the Wacs who ran the switchboards of the next war.

1918 – 1919

Chief Operator Helen Cook’s uniform

The National Museum of American History keeps the uniform of Helen Cook, a chief operator of the Signal Corps telephone units in France: a navy wool coat and skirt and an overseas cap. It carries the patch of the AEF General Headquarters, a chief operator’s insignia, two overseas service chevrons and a Victory ribbon with a silver citation star.

More than 400 women trained as operators. Those who served overseas were recognized as veterans only in 1979.

April 12, 1944

Wacs on the Fifth Army’s switchboards

In Italy, women of the 6669th WAC Headquarters Platoon ran the switchboards of the Fifth Army. Signal Corps photographs name Pfc. Zenaida Johnson of New York and Pfc. Berthe Audet of Manchester, New Hampshire. A generation after the Hello Girls, women again kept the Army’s lines open overseas.

Sources: National Museum of American History, Helen Cook uniform, 1998.0165.35.01; Truman Library, photograph 2014-3268, NAID 348666273 and related photographs.
