The Network in the Sand

August 1990 to February 1991. When the first Americans reached Saudi Arabia, the U.S. military had two leased telephone circuits there. Seven months later, Signal Soldiers were running what the Army's lessons-learned center called "the largest automatic switched voice and message network in the history of the U.S. Army."

From two circuits to a theater

Iraq invaded Kuwait on 2 August 1990. Within days the United States began moving forces to Saudi Arabia, and the Signal Corps faced a country with almost no military communications to build on. Army historian Rebecca Raines records that "the U.S. military had just two leased telephone circuits and two record traffic circuits" there when the buildup began.

The 11th Signal Brigade, the Army's theater signal brigade, arrived from Fort Huachuca, Arizona, and began building. Raines writes that by the end of August it "was running the largest common user data communications system ever present in a theater of operations." Its own 40th and 86th Signal Battalions were joined by the 44th and 63d from Germany and by the 67th Signal Battalion from Fort Gordon, until the brigade had five signal battalions and two companies.

Because military satellites were scarce, the Army also leased circuits on commercial satellites. Military satellite terminals of the Defense Satellite Communications System grew from seven at the start to 128 at their peak, and Army space historians credit DSCS with carrying about half of all the theater's communications.

As the force grew to two Army corps, so did the network. The 35th Signal Brigade came from Fort Bragg with XVIII Airborne Corps. The 93d Signal Brigade came from Germany with VII Corps, its equipment scattered "among twenty different ships." On 4 December 1990 the Army activated the 6th Signal Command at Fort Huachuca to administer the theater network. Its commander, Charles G. Sutten Jr., was the overall commander of all Army signal units in Operation Desert Storm.

By the time the ground war began, "fifteen voice and five message switches supplied communications support to more than ninety locations." Air Force Lieutenant General James Cassity, the Joint Staff's communications director, summed it up: "We put more electronic communications connectivity into the Gulf in 90 days than we put in Europe in 40 years."

Satellites and the link home

Military satellites of the Defense Satellite Communications System (DSCS) carried about half of the theater's traffic. Terminals grew from seven ground-mobile force terminals to 128 at the peak. A RAND study counted 110 terminals on 15 January 1991 and "50 major network configuration changes" during the operation.

"Due to a shortage of military satellites, the Army leased circuits from commercial satellites." The 35th Signal Brigade kept a permanent satellite link to its home at Fort Bragg.

The theater backbone

The 11th Signal Brigade, the Army's theater signal brigade from Fort Huachuca, built the backbone behind both corps: telephone and message switches, the data network and long-haul radio links. It grew to five signal battalions and two companies, including the 67th Signal Battalion from Fort Gordon.

The 6th Signal Command, activated 4 December 1990, took on the job of administering the theater network. The Florida Army National Guard's 653d Signal Company added troposcatter radio links, which bounce signals off the lower atmosphere to reach beyond the horizon.

The Army's lessons-learned center found that eight organizations planned and managed parts of the switched network, from Central Command down to the three signal brigades.

35th Signal Brigade with XVIII Airborne Corps

From Fort Bragg, the brigade gave the corps "communications coverage over an area of more than 120,000 square miles in northern Saudi Arabia and southern Iraq." It "installed 169 separate communications systems as well as 400 miles of wire and cable." Its battalions included the 25th, the 50th (Airborne), the 327th and the 426th, joined by elements of the 57th Signal Battalion.

XVIII Airborne Corps made the longest move of the war, over 500 miles west to Rafha, and the brigade's network had to move with it.

93d Signal Brigade with VII Corps

VII Corps came from Germany, and its signal brigade's equipment was dispersed "among twenty different ships." The brigade linked the corps headquarters, five divisions and an armored cavalry regiment, but only two of those five divisions had finished fielding Mobile Subscriber Equipment (MSE) before the war.

After the war the brigade's colors came home to Fort Gordon, where it was inactivated on 5 December 1991.

Division signal battalions

Mobile Subscriber Equipment, the Army's new tactical radiotelephone network, "received its battle testing in the harsh desert environment." The 3d Armored Division planned to "drop off the node centers every 30 - 35 km" behind its lead brigade as it advanced.

When the attack outran the nodes, satellites carried the load. The 24th Infantry Division's signal officer reported that "single channel TACSAT was the only means of long-distance communications during most of the attack into Iraq."

The Soldier at the end of the line

The new SINCGARS radio "achieved a mean time between failures rate of 7,000 hours," against 200 to 300 hours for the radio it replaced. An e-mail system let soldiers write to family and friends, and it handled "approximately fifteen thousand such messages each day."

Everyone wanted to call home. The 24th Infantry Division's signal officer reported "near grid lock on the military telephone system" until two Saudi telephone banks and two AT&T phone banks were installed.

The shift west, and the hundred hours

The ground plan depended on two corps moving west in secret, and on their signal brigades moving the network with them. Switch between the two phases.

The shift "began on 20 January, and the movement continued unabated for about three weeks." The Army gathered almost 4,000 heavy trucks for it, and at its height "about eighteen vehicles per minute passed along any given stretch of road." Each corps signal brigade had to pick up its network and rebuild it at the far end of the move.

The ground war opened on 24 February. As VII Corps attacked, the 3d Armored Division planned to drop off an MSE node center every 30 to 35 kilometers to keep its command posts connected. Kuwait City was liberated on 27 February, and the cease-fire took effect "at 0800, 28 February."

Building it, month by month

  1. 2 August 1990
    Iraq invades Kuwait

    The U.S. military has two leased telephone circuits and two record traffic circuits in Saudi Arabia.

  2. August 1990
    The first Guard signal unit

    The South Carolina Army National Guard's 228th Signal Detachment becomes the first National Guard unit sent to the Persian Gulf.

  3. End of August 1990
    A theater data network

    The 11th Signal Brigade is running "the largest common user data communications system ever present in a theater of operations."

  4. 22 September 1990
    Fort Gordon's battalion is alerted

    The 67th Signal Battalion, stationed at Fort Gordon, is alerted for Southwest Asia and goes under the operational control of the 11th Signal Brigade.

  5. November 1990
    VII Corps is ordered from Germany

    Its 93d Signal Brigade ships out with its equipment spread across twenty ships. The 51st Signal Battalion, alerted 8 November, has people on the ground in Saudi Arabia within three weeks.

  6. 4 December 1990
    6th Signal Command activated

    The Army activates the 6th Signal Command at Fort Huachuca to administer the theater network.

  7. 6 January 1991
    Troposcatter from Florida

    The Florida Guard's 653d Signal Company, mobilized 6 December and the largest reserve-component signal unit sent to the theater, arrives. It reaches Camp Thunderbird three hours before the air war begins.

  8. 15 January 1991
    110 satellite terminals

    RAND counts 110 DSCS terminals on the network, sharing 67.65 megabits per second of satellite capacity.

  9. 17 January 1991
    The air war begins

    Operation Desert Shield becomes Operation Desert Storm.

  10. 20 January 1991
    The shift west

    Both corps begin moving west, XVIII Airborne Corps over 500 miles and VII Corps over 330. Their networks move with them.

  11. 24 February 1991
    The ground war

    Mobile Subscriber Equipment gets its first combat test, with single-channel satellite radio carrying long-distance traffic as the attack outruns the nodes.

  12. 28 February 1991
    Cease-fire

    The cease-fire takes effect at 0800, one hundred hours after the ground war began.

  13. March 1991
    Staying on

    The 54th Signal Battalion is formed to provide information management support for the theater.

  14. 5 December 1991
    Colors home to Fort Gordon

    The 93d Signal Brigade's colors return to Fort Gordon for the brigade's inactivation.

The Fort Gordon connection

"In 1990-1991, the Signal Center played a vital role in preparing soldiers for deployment during Desert Shield/Desert Storm," the Cyber Center of Excellence history records. Fort Gordon sent the 67th Signal Battalion to the war and received the 93d Signal Brigade's colors when it came home. One of the captains who built networks for the 101st Airborne Division in the war's great air assault, Alan R. Lynn, came back to Fort Gordon twenty years later as Chief of Signal.

What the desert taught

New equipment proved itself

MSE and SINCGARS both had their first war in the desert, and both held up. SINCGARS failed about once in 7,000 hours, against 200 to 300 hours for the radio it replaced.

Satellites became essential

DSCS carried about half the theater's traffic, and single-channel TACSAT was often the only long-distance link once the attack began.

Not everything fit together

Raines notes that with new and older systems mixed in one network, "data transmission remained problematical." Reserve units without compatible equipment were left off the force list, and the official history records that "American field radios proved unreliable" for some units in the attack.
