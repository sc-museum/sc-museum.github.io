Combat Camera & Video

For more than a century, the Signal Corps has been the Army's camera. It documented the fight, trained the troops, and brought some of Hollywood's biggest names into uniform to do it.

The Army's eye

Photography became a Signal Corps mission in the 1880s, and the Corps has held it ever since. Signal Corps photographers covered the American Expeditionary Forces in France in the First World War. In the Second, the Corps opened its own film studio, the Signal Corps Photographic Center in Astoria, New York, and sent photographic detachments to every theater of the war.

Film was a training tool as much as a record. The Signal Corps Pictorial Service made hundreds of training films, and it recruited the people who knew the craft best: directors, writers, animators, and studio executives. Two of them are profiled below from the museum's own fact sheets. Today that lineage lives on in the Army's combat camera Soldiers, who still document operations as they happen.

Hollywood in uniform

Two storytellers who served in the Signal Corps during World War II.

Lt. Col. Theodor Seuss "Ted" Geisel
Dr. Seuss · Signal Corps Pictorial Service

Born March 2, 1904, in Massachusetts, Geisel was a children's author, political cartoonist, illustrator, poet, animator, screenwriter, and filmmaker. He wrote most of his books under the pen name Dr. Seuss, and The Cat in the Hat is the best known of them.

In World War II he served with the Signal Corps Pictorial Service making training films, alongside director Frank Capra (Mr. Smith Goes to Washington, It's a Wonderful Life), animation directors Chuck Jones and Friz Freleng, voice artist Mel Blanc, and music director Carl Stalling. Geisel spoke out against racism at home toward Jews and Black Americans, which he saw as harming the war effort, and some of his early wartime cartoons show it.

Capra came up with Private Snafu, one of the most successful animated characters of the war. Geisel led the animation section and wrote most of the 26 episodes produced between 1943 and 1945. Snafu was a Soldier who usually did the wrong thing out of laziness or ignorance, and troops were meant to learn from his mistakes. The cartoons managed to be funny and instructive at the same time.

Geisel left the Army as a lieutenant colonel and received the Legion of Merit for his wartime service. He died on September 24, 1991, in La Jolla, California. His birthday, March 2, is now National Read Across America Day.

Col. Darryl F. Zanuck
Producer, The Longest Day · Signal Corps

Zanuck was born in Nebraska in 1902 and was working in films by age eight. At fifteen he lied about his age to join the Nebraska National Guard and served on the Mexican border and in France during World War I. At Warner Brothers he wrote Rin Tin Tin pictures, helped the studio switch to sound with The Jazz Singer (1927), and became chief of production, turning out hits like Little Caesar and The Public Enemy. He co-founded Twentieth Century Pictures in 1933, which merged with Fox in 1935 to form Twentieth Century Fox. There he produced The Grapes of Wrath and How Green Was My Valley.

The Army commissioned him a colonel in the Signal Corps in 1942. As a military observer in London, he negotiated agreements with the British Ministry of Information and the Soviet Film Bureau to coordinate information and training films among the Allies. On August 19, 1942, he went along on the Allied raid at Dieppe. He then supervised Report from the Aleutians, the first film in a War Department historical series, which won a New York Film Critics award.

For the next film in the series, At the Front in North Africa, Zanuck organized special photographic detachments to cover the amphibious landings. Nothing in it was staged, and every combat scene was real. The work earned him the Legion of Merit. He supervised filming in Italy before returning to the studio in 1944.

In 1962 he released The Longest Day, the D-Day epic many consider his greatest film. It was shot in Corsica and Normandy with forty-eight technical advisors and a cast that included John Wayne, Robert Mitchum, Henry Fonda, Richard Burton, and Sean Connery. It cost $10 million and earned $40 million at the box office.

As head of Twentieth Century Fox he went on to oversee The Sound of Music (1965), Planet of the Apes (1968), and Tora! Tora! Tora! (1970). He died in Palm Springs, California, in 1979.

Academy Awards won by the Signal Corps

Signal Corps films won Oscars as well as the war's information campaign. The first issue of the Society's Heritage magazine made them its cover story.

"The Signal Corps Pictorial Center, Astoria, NY, 1942–1970": an exhibit case with an officer's uniform, a motion picture camera, a citation, and two Academy Award statuettes.
Academy Award · 1942

Prelude to War

Best Documentary. Directed by Frank Capra, it told soldiers and the public what the war was about and the events that led to it.

Academy Award · 1946

Seeds of Destiny

Best Documentary Short Subject. It made the case for rebuilding war-torn Europe and helped inform the public about what became the Marshall Plan.

From the Archives: The Corps Behind the Camera

More of the Signal Corps photographers and filmmakers, from the National Archives and the Smithsonian.

July 21, 1917 – 1918

Shooting the Great War

The Signal Corps was given the Army’s photographic work on July 21, 1917. Its Photographic Section with the American Expeditionary Forces grew from 25 men in August 1917 to 92 officers and 498 men by November 1918.

Its cameramen shot 589,197 feet of motion picture film in France, developed in laboratories at St. Ouen and then Vincennes. A surviving instruction sheet for Signal Corps photographers warns that film could stick in France’s damp climate.

1918

Carl Louis Gregory, teacher of the cameramen

Lt. Carl Louis Gregory was chief instructor at the Signal Corps School of Photography at Columbia University, where he designed the courses that trained the Army’s cameramen. In 1932 he served on the committee whose advice on film storage shaped how the new National Archives would keep motion picture film.

About 1918

A Signal Corps motion picture camera

The National Museum of American History keeps a 35mm Burke & James motion picture camera used by the Signal Corps around 1918, with its wooden case, brass gears and olive-drab film magazines. It is not on display.

1917 – 1918

Edward Steichen and the camera in the air

The photographer Edward Steichen enlisted in July 1917, at 38, and joined the Army’s new photographic work under Maj. James Barnes. In August 1918 he took charge of the Photographic Section, backed the school of aerial photography at Tours, and in October was promoted to major. The National Air and Space Museum keeps his section’s records.

Smithsonian sources differ on whose section it was: one says it began under the Signal Corps before passing to the Air Service; the museum’s finding aid names only the Air Service.

1918

The 92nd Division, through the Signal Corps lens

Signal Corps photographers followed the 92nd Division through France. Their prints, now at the National Museum of African American History and Culture, still carry the “Signal Corps USA” mark and negative numbers. One shows men captured by the division at St. Dié, in the Vosges, on September 14, 1918; others show the 366th and 367th Infantry between August and October 1918.

1944 – 1945

Sgt. Irene Marquardt

A professional photographer from Iowa before the war, Irene Marquardt enlisted in the Women’s Army Auxiliary Corps and served with the Army Pictorial Service. Captions of the day called her the only Wac photographer in the European Theater. She photographed fellow Wacs in England and France, including the first WAC detachment to land in France.

April 29, 1945

Witness at Dachau

Signal Corps cameramen with the 42nd and 45th Infantry Divisions and the 20th Armored Division filmed the Dachau concentration camp on the day of its liberation. The National Archives holds 228 reels of that footage, about 200,000 feet. The production file of “Death Mills” shows how the Signal Corps and the Army Pictorial Service cleared it for American audiences in 1947.

1947 – 1960

Korea’s refugees on film

Signal Corps crews recorded Korea’s refugees across thirteen years: a refugee train arriving at Baik Chung in April 1947, people crossing the Han River beside its destroyed bridge, soldiers of the 65th Infantry from Puerto Rico carrying refugees by truck in March 1951, and refugee housing in Seoul in 1960.

1950

Lt. Frank Winslow in Korea

Photographs at the Truman Library show Lt. Frank Winslow, a Signal Corps photographer, filming near the front in the first weeks of the Korean War. Another record names him as the photo officer of the Korean Military Advisory Group, from Bangor, Maine.

1961 – 1965

Cecil Stoughton in the White House

Cecil Stoughton was a Signal Corps officer assigned to the White House Army Signal Agency, and the first official White House photographer. In President Kennedy’s years he made more than 8,000 photographs of the Kennedy family. On November 22, 1963, he was the only photographer aboard Air Force One when Lyndon B. Johnson took the oath of office.

Sources: National Archives, The Unwritten Record, Shooting World War I; Carl Louis Gregory; Sergeant Irene Marquardt; How Death Mills came to U.S. audiences and Korean War refugees in Signal Corps films; National Museum of American History, Signal Corps motion picture camera, PG.006533C; National Air and Space Museum Archives, Edward Steichen AEF Photo Section collection; Air & Space/Smithsonian, Steichen’s Navy; National Museum of African American History and Culture, 92nd Division print 2015.97.23.18; Truman Library, photograph 2007-706 and 2017-104; JFK Library, Cecil Stoughton oral history and swearing-in photograph, NAID 194235. Sources give Stoughton’s rank as captain and as major, and the Winslow records disagree on his unit.
