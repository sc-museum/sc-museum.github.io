The Coding and Encryption Annex

From the cipher disk to the Enigma to artificial intelligence: how the Army keeps a message secret.

Why codes matter

Keeping the message secret

Every message an army sends can be overheard. A flag can be read from the next hill. A radio signal crosses the front line. A message on a network can be copied anywhere along its path. Getting the message through is half of the Signal Corps' work. Keeping it from the enemy is the other half.

A cipher scrambles a message with a secret setting, called a key, so that only someone holding the same key can read it. The Signal Corps has done this since the Civil War, when it developed a cipher disk of two concentric disks so that Confederate observers could not read its flag signals. The Army's historian records that the enemy apparently never broke it. The story of those signals is in the .

The same idea now guards far more than battlefield orders. In the words of the National Institute of Standards and Technology, encryption provides “security and privacy for just about everything we do online.” Protecting information, and the networks that carry it, is the daily work of the soldiers trained at Fort Gordon.

The machine

The Enigma

Arthur Scherbius, a German engineer, offered a prototype cipher machine to the German Navy in April 1918. The Navy adopted the Enigma in February 1926 and the German Army in July 1928. It looked like a typewriter in a wooden box.

Pressing a key sent an electric current through a plugboard, through three wired rotors, off a reflecting plate, and back through the rotors and plugboard to light one lamp. That lamp was the enciphered letter. One rotor turned with every key press, so the same letter came out differently each time. To read a message, the receiver set a second machine to the same starting position and typed in the scrambled text.

A National Security Agency mathematician put the number of ways the wartime machine could be set at about one hundred thousand billion billion. It had one flaw that codebreakers used: the Enigma could never encipher a letter as itself.

Breaking it

In December 1932 three Polish mathematicians, Marian Rejewski, Henryk Zygalski and Jerzy Różycki, worked out the machine's wiring and began reading its messages. In late July 1939, weeks before the invasion of Poland, they shared the secret with French and British codebreakers. At Bletchley Park in England, Alan Turing, Gordon Welchman and the engineer Harold Keen designed the Bombe, a machine that searched for each day's settings. The first working Bombes arrived in August 1940.

The United States joined the effort. The Navy contracted the National Cash Register Company of Dayton, Ohio, in March 1942, and the engineer Joseph Desch led the design. The first two machines, called Adam and Eve, were finished about May 1, 1943. Dayton built 121 Bombes, run by about 600 Navy WAVES and 200 sailors. The Army had its own built by Bell Laboratories, and from mid-1943 American Army officers worked beside the British at Bletchley Park.

1860s to 1952

Code makers and code breakers

Civil War

The cipher disk

The Signal Corps developed two concentric disks of letters and numbers to protect its flag signals.

June 10, 1917

MI-8

The Army established its Code and Cipher Section, known as MI-8, two months after the United States entered World War I.

1917–1918

William Friedman at Riverbank

At Riverbank Laboratories in Illinois, William F. Friedman taught three six-week courses in codebreaking for Army officers. He was commissioned a first lieutenant in May 1918 and assigned to the Radio Intelligence Section in France.

1918

River and Lake Codes

In France, a Code Compilation Section in the office of the chief signal officer devised the River and Lake Codes for the First and Second Armies.

October 26, 1918

The first code talkers

Choctaw soldiers of the 142nd Infantry Regiment, 36th Division, passed messages in their own language in the Meuse-Argonne campaign. The attack that followed took the Germans by surprise.

1929–1930

The Signal Intelligence Service

The Chief Signal Officer established the Signal Intelligence Service to control all Army cryptology, with Friedman as its chief. In April 1930 he hired its first three junior cryptanalysts: Frank Rowlett, Abraham Sinkov and Solomon Kullback.

1935–1941

SIGABA

Friedman and Rowlett designed a cipher machine whose rotors stepped irregularly. The Army fielded its first 459 machines in June 1941 and held more than 10,000 by the end of the war. The National Security Agency calls it the only machine system of World War II that no enemy broke.

September 1940

Purple, and MAGIC

Under Friedman's direction and Rowlett's daily lead, the Service broke the Japanese diplomatic cipher machine the Americans called Purple. The key insight came from cryptanalyst Genevieve Grotjan. Engineer Leo Rosen built a working copy of a machine no American had seen. The first translations were handed in on September 27, 1940. The intelligence was codenamed MAGIC.

1941–1944

Comanche code talkers

Seventeen Comanche soldiers of the 4th Signal Company, 4th Infantry Division, built a code from their language. Thirteen landed at Utah Beach on D-Day. Their story, and their time at Camp Gordon, is told in . The Navajo code talkers of the Pacific were Marines.

1942

Arlington Hall

The Army acquired Arlington Hall, a former school in Virginia, in June 1942, and moved its codebreaking headquarters there in August.

February 1, 1943

Venona

Gene Grabeel began work at Arlington Hall on Soviet diplomatic messages, the project later called Venona.

July 15, 1943

SIGSALY

The first secure voice system went into use with a conference between the Pentagon and London. Bell Telephone Laboratories designed it, and the Signal Corps formed the 805th Signal Service Company to run its terminals around the world.

1945–1952

From the Army to NSA

The Army's Signal Security Agency became the Army Security Agency on September 15, 1945. The Armed Forces Security Agency followed on May 20, 1949, and the National Security Agency on November 4, 1952.

1976 to today

From machines to mathematics, and to artificial intelligence

After the war the rotors gave way to mathematics run on computers. The strength of a cipher no longer rests on hiding how the machine works. The method is published, tested by anyone who cares to attack it, and only the key stays secret.

1976

Public-key cryptography

A new kind of cipher let two people who had never met agree on a secret over an open line. The National Institute of Standards and Technology dates its invention to 1976.

1977

The Data Encryption Standard

The National Bureau of Standards published the Data Encryption Standard as a federal standard, FIPS 46.

November 26, 2001

The Advanced Encryption Standard

Its replacement was published as FIPS 197.

September 7, 2022

Preparing for quantum computers

The National Security Agency told national security systems to move to quantum-resistant algorithms, warning that a powerful enough quantum computer could break the public-key systems in use today.

August 13, 2024

Post-quantum standards

The National Institute of Standards and Technology released the first three encryption standards designed to withstand a quantum computer: FIPS 203, 204 and 205.

What artificial intelligence is changing

The public record shows artificial intelligence changing this field in two ways. It is a new thing to protect, and it is a new tool for the defenders.

2023–2024

Securing AI itself

On September 28, 2023, the Director of the National Security Agency announced an Artificial Intelligence Security Center to oversee the secure development and use of AI in national security systems. Its first guidance, on deploying AI systems securely, came out with allied partners on April 15, 2024.

September 2024

A roadmap for cyber operations

U.S. Cyber Command presented a five-year AI roadmap for cyber operations with more than one hundred activities, led by a task force in its Cyber National Mission Force.

July 2025

AI at Army Cyber Command

Army Cyber Command reported a twelve-month pilot of an AI platform for cyberspace operations, overseen by an AI governance board. Its commander at the time, Lt. Gen. Maria Barrett, said: “AI is not a luxury — it's a necessity.”

The commands

U.S. Army Cyber Command

U.S. Cyber Command began with a memo from the Secretary of Defense on June 23, 2009. It reached initial operating capability on May 21, 2010, at Fort Meade, Maryland, beside the National Security Agency, and became a full unified combatant command on May 4, 2018.

The Army's part of that force, U.S. Army Cyber Command, was activated on October 1, 2010, under Lt. Gen. Rhett A. Hernandez, in a ceremony at Fort Belvoir, Virginia. It conducts cyberspace operations with the Army's other forces: it operates and defends Army networks and can act against an adversary's.

In December 2013 the Army announced that the command would move to Fort Gordon. Ground was broken on November 29, 2016. The command cased its colors at Fort Belvoir on July 1, 2020, and uncased them at Fort Gordon on July 24. On September 3, 2020, it dedicated its headquarters, Fortitude Hall: 336,000 square feet, about $366 million, and named for Operation Fortitude, the deception that hid the Normandy landings. Where the Army's cyber force lives

Mid-1990s

NSA arrives

The National Security Agency came to Fort Gordon when 50 people arrived to set up one of its regional operations centers.

July 22, 2008

7th Signal Command

The 7th Signal Command, first activated in 1975, was reactivated at Fort Gordon.

March 5, 2012

The Georgia Cryptologic Center

The National Security Agency opened the NSA/CSS Georgia Cryptologic Center on post, a $286 million complex. One of its buildings is named for John Whitelaw, the first operations chief of NSA Georgia.

2014

The Cyber Center of Excellence

The Signal Center of Excellence became the Army Cyber Center of Excellence, bringing signal and cyber training under one command.

August 4, 2014

The Cyber School

The U.S. Army Cyber School unveiled its headquarters, with Col. Jennifer Buckner as its first commandant. Its first officer course began on August 6, 2015.

September 1, 2014

The Cyber branch

The Army established the Cyber branch, with its home at Fort Gordon.

September 5, 2014

The Cyber Protection Brigade

The Cyber Protection Brigade, the first of its kind in the Army, was activated on Barton Field.

July 10, 2018

The Georgia Cyber Center

In downtown Augusta, the Georgia Cyber Center opened its first building, the Hull McKnight Building.

September 3, 2020

Fortitude Hall

Army Cyber Command dedicated Fortitude Hall, bringing its headquarters to Fort Gordon.
