The Weather Annex

From Army surgeons' weather diaries to the forecast on your phone: from 1870 to 1891 the Signal Corps was the nation's weather service, and it kept working on Army weather long after.

The line of descent

The Signal Service years

1814–1873

Army doctors keep the first weather diaries

The Army was recording weather long before it had a weather service. In 1814 the Surgeon General had Army post and hospital surgeons keep weather diaries, because doctors wanted to understand how weather and disease were connected. The National Archives calls this the first nationwide federal weather system. The Smithsonian began gathering weather reports from volunteer observers in 1847, and in 1873 those observers were handed over to the Signal Corps.

1870

Congress hands the job to the Army

In 1870 a series of freak storms killed several people on the Great Lakes. Congress responded with a law, approved February 9, 1870, directing the War Department to take weather observations at military posts and share them by telegraph. A Wisconsin congressman, Halbert Paine, wrote the measure and argued that military discipline would give the quickest, most regular and most accurate reports. On March 15, 1870, the job went to the Chief Signal Officer, Albert J. Myer, and the Signal Corps became the nation's weather service.

1870–1880

Myer builds the network

Myer trained signalmen to run the new service, and soldiers took observations at stations across the country, with reports sent by telegraph. According to the Army's concise history, there were 25 regular stations in 1870 and 60 the next year, and one of them was in Augusta, Georgia. By the time Myer died in 1880 the service was world renowned. In the 1880s weather work took up nearly all of the Signal Corps' effort.

1880–1881

From Myer to the Arctic

Myer died in 1880. In 1881 Lt. Adolphus Greely led a Signal Corps party to the Arctic to take part in the First International Polar Year. Their station at Lady Franklin Bay, which the Army also calls Fort Conger, was the northernmost polar site that year. Greely later supervised the handover of the weather service to the Department of Agriculture.

1890–1891

The weather goes civilian

In 1890 Congress decided the nation's weather service should be civilian. President Benjamin Harrison signed the law on October 1, 1890, creating the Weather Bureau in the Department of Agriculture. The Signal Corps' weather network moved to the new bureau, and the change took effect on July 1, 1891. The Army sources say the Signal Corps kept responsibility for military weather work, and by then it operated about five hundred observation stations.

Weather for the Army

1917–1918

The Army builds a Meteorological Service for the war

When the United States entered World War I, the Army needed weather forecasts for aviators, artillery and gas attacks. In September 1917 Maj. William R. Blair went to France to organize a Signal Corps Meteorological Service. The first American weather station in France opened in May 1918, and by October 1918 there were twenty-two. By the end of the war the Army had trained about five hundred weathermen.

1919–1937

Weather moves to the Air Corps

After the war most of the trained weathermen left the Army. Capt. Randolph “Pinkie” Williams argued for a permanent military weather service, and in 1935 the Chief Signal Officer expanded the Corps' Meteorological Section. In 1937 a committee led by the Secretary of War recommended that the Air Corps run weather service in wartime. On July 1, 1937, the Meteorological Section moved from the Signal Corps to the Army Air Corps, which created the Army Air Corps Weather Service. The Signal Corps kept the job of developing and supplying weather equipment. In 1947, when the Air Force became a separate service, Air Force Weather took over weather support for both the Air Force and the Army.

1940s–1961

The Signal Corps' instruments reach space

Even after the weather forecasters left, the Signal Corps kept building the instruments. In the 1940s its laboratories used radar to track storms. On April 1, 1960, the first TIROS weather satellite was launched. The Signal Corps provided its meteorological instruments, including the television cameras, and Fort Monmouth hosted one of the two ground stations that received its pictures. TIROS-1 worked for 78 days and sent back nearly 23,000 cloud pictures, showing for the first time that satellites could survey the world's weather. 1940–1970

What became of the Weather Bureau

The Weather Bureau that grew out of the Signal Corps stayed civilian. It moved to the Department of Commerce in 1940, became part of the Environmental Science Services Administration in 1965, and in 1970 joined the new National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. The National Weather Service inside NOAA is its direct successor, so the forecast on your phone traces back to Army signalmen taking observations in 1870.
